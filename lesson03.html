Day 2, Afternoon • Domain 2A

Risk Identification

You cannot analyze a risk you have not described. Risk identification turns a vague worry into a specific, testable statement: who or what acts, what happens, to which asset, and over what time. This lesson covers risk events and factors, threat actors and the threat landscape, threat modeling, vulnerability management with CVSS v4.0, EPSS, and the CISA KEV catalog, and the craft of building and evaluating risk scenarios.

Session: Day 2, 1:00 to 4:30 Duration: ~3.5 hrs Exam Weight: 22% Knowledge Check: 8 Questions

Overview

From worry to scenario

Domain 2 begins with identification because every later step depends on it. A risk that is never named is never analyzed, never owned, and never treated. A risk that is named badly, such as “cyberattack” or “IT failure,” cannot be measured or assigned. Good identification draws on threat intelligence, threat modeling, and vulnerability data, and it ends in risk scenarios that business owners recognize as real.

The running case is Brightwater Credit Union, a member-owned financial cooperative with a core banking platform, a wire-transfer operation, and online banking hosted in a cloud tenant. Brightwater is worried about ransomware and wire fraud. By the end of the lesson you will turn those worries into scenarios it can analyze in Lesson 4.

⚖️ Think like a risk practitionerStart from what the business is trying to achieve, then ask what could stop it. A vulnerability scan tells you what is broken. A risk scenario tells the business what that could cost them. When a question asks what drives scenario development, business objectives come before tools and scan results.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Distinguish risk events, internal and external risk factors, threats, and vulnerabilities. Bloom: Understand
  • Characterize threat actors and select threat intelligence sources suited to an organization’s sector. Bloom: Apply
  • Apply STRIDE, attack trees, and MITRE ATT&CK to identify threats to a business process. Bloom: Apply
  • Prioritize vulnerabilities by combining CVSS v4.0, EPSS, CISA KEV status, and asset criticality. Bloom: Analyze
  • Construct and evaluate risk scenarios from top-down and bottom-up inputs, using the five scenario components. Bloom: Evaluate

Risk Events and Risk Factors

A risk event is the thing that happens: a system goes down, data leaks, money moves to the wrong account. A risk factor is a condition that makes the event more or less likely, or more or less harmful. Brightwater’s aging core banking platform is a risk factor. A ransomware outage of that platform is a risk event.

Separating the two keeps the register clean. Factors are monitored and often become key risk indicators. Events are what scenarios describe and what analysis quantifies.

Internal risk factorsExternal risk factors
Strategy and change: new products, mergers, migrationsThreat landscape: active criminal campaigns, new exploit techniques
Technology age, complexity, and technical debtRegulation and supervision: new rules, examination findings
Staffing, skills, turnover, and key-person dependencySuppliers and fourth parties: concentration and failures
Process maturity and control effectivenessEconomic and market conditions
Risk culture and tone at the topGeopolitical events, natural hazards, and climate

Sources for identifying risk

  • Internal data: incident and near-miss logs, audit findings, control test results, help desk trends, and loss history.
  • People: interviews and workshops with process owners, who know where their processes break.
  • Structured techniques: checklists, SWOT, root cause analysis, and the threat modeling methods in Section 3.
  • External data: threat intelligence, regulator guidance, industry loss data, and peer incidents.
💡 Exam TipA vulnerability alone is not a risk. Risk needs a threat that can exploit the vulnerability and an impact on something the business values. If an answer choice treats a scan finding as the risk itself, look for a better option that ties it to a threat and an objective.

Threats, Threat Actors, and the Threat Landscape

A threat is anything that can cause harm by exploiting a weakness. The actor is the source. Knowing the actor tells you about motive, capability, and likely methods, which in turn shapes likelihood.

ActorTypical motiveCredit union example
Cybercriminal groupMoneyRansomware against the core platform; business email compromise aimed at wires
Malicious insiderMoney, grievance, coercionA teller who sells member data
Careless or mistaken employeeNone (accidental)A misdirected email containing member statements
Supplier or third partyVaries; often failure or compromiseA core processor outage or a breach at a card processor
Nation-stateEspionage, disruptionRarely targets a regional credit union directly, but may hit shared infrastructure
HacktivistIdeology, publicityDenial of service against online banking
Natural eventNoneA flood that takes out a branch or a data center

Threat intelligence

  • Strategic intelligence: trends and motives for leadership, such as which sectors ransomware groups are targeting.
  • Operational intelligence: campaigns and adversary methods, useful for planning defenses and scenarios.
  • Tactical and technical intelligence: indicators of compromise and specific techniques for security operations.
  • Sources: Information Sharing and Analysis Centers such as FS-ISAC for financial services, government advisories such as those from CISA, vendor research, open-source intelligence, and the organization’s own incidents.

3

Threat Modeling

Threat modeling asks a structured question of a system or process: what can go wrong here? It works best early in design, but it also helps when assessing an existing process. The usual inputs are a data flow diagram, the trust boundaries where data crosses from one level of trust to another, and the assets that matter.

STRIDE threatProperty violatedWire-transfer example
SpoofingAuthenticationAn attacker emails as a member and requests a wire
TamperingIntegrityBeneficiary account details are changed in transit
RepudiationNonrepudiationAn employee denies approving a wire, and logs cannot prove otherwise
Information disclosureConfidentialityWire instructions are visible to all staff in a shared mailbox
Denial of serviceAvailabilityThe wire system is unavailable at the daily cutoff
Elevation of privilegeAuthorizationA clerk gains approver rights and releases wires alone

Other methods

  • Attack trees: the attacker’s goal is the root (for example, “send a fraudulent wire”), and each branch is a way to reach it. Trees show which paths are cheapest for the attacker and where one control blocks several branches.
  • MITRE ATT&CK: a knowledge base of real adversary tactics, from Reconnaissance and Initial Access through Lateral Movement to Exfiltration and Impact, with the techniques used to achieve each. Use it to check whether scenarios reflect how attackers actually behave and whether detection covers the techniques that matter.
  • Misuse cases: write the abuse version of each use case. “Member requests a wire” becomes “attacker requests a wire as the member.”
🧭 From model to scenarioEach credible threat path in a model is a candidate bottom-up scenario. The model gives you the actor, the event, and the asset. The business supplies the impact and the time dimension.
💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks when threat modeling gives the MOST value, choose the design phase of a new system or major change. Fixing a design flaw on paper costs far less than fixing it in production.
4

Vulnerability Management

Vulnerability management is a continuous cycle, not a quarterly scan. Its value to the risk practitioner is evidence: which weaknesses exist, how exploitable they are, and whether they are fixed on time.

  • Inventory: know the assets first. A scanner cannot assess what it does not know exists.
  • Discover: authenticated and unauthenticated scans, agent-based assessment, cloud configuration checks, software composition analysis, and penetration tests.
  • Prioritize: combine severity, exploitation evidence, and business context, as shown below.
  • Remediate: patch, reconfigure, or apply a compensating control, within service-level targets set by policy.
  • Verify and report: rescan to confirm, track overdue items and approved exceptions, and report trends as KRIs.

Three signals, three different questions

SignalOwnerQuestion it answersNotes
CVSS v4.0FIRSTHow severe is this vulnerability technically?Released November 2023. Metric groups: Base, Threat, Environmental, Supplemental. Scores are labeled by the groups used, such as CVSS-B (Base only) or CVSS-BTE. Ratings: Low 0.1 to 3.9, Medium 4.0 to 6.9, High 7.0 to 8.9, Critical 9.0 to 10.0.
EPSSFIRSTHow likely is exploitation activity in the next 30 days?A probability from 0 to 1 with a percentile, updated daily from observed data. It estimates likelihood, not impact.
CISA KEV catalogCISAIs it being exploited right now?Lists vulnerabilities with reliable evidence of active exploitation. Binding Operational Directive 22-01 requires U.S. federal civilian agencies to remediate listed items by set due dates. Many other organizations use it as a priority signal.

An example prioritization rule

The following is an illustrative internal policy, not a standard. Each organization sets its own targets based on appetite and capacity.

ConditionExample target
On the KEV catalog and on an internet-facing or critical assetRemediate or isolate within days
High EPSS probability, or Critical CVSS on a critical assetRemediate within two weeks
High CVSS, low EPSS, internal assetRemediate within the normal monthly cycle
Low severity and low exploitation likelihoodNext scheduled maintenance, or accept with an owner’s sign-off
⚠️ Common mistakeTreating the CVSS Base score as a risk rating. CVSS measures technical severity. It says nothing about whether attackers are using the flaw, what the affected system does for the business, or which controls already reduce exposure.
💡 Exam TipWhen asked how to BEST prioritize a large backlog of vulnerabilities, choose the answer that combines exploitability and business impact (asset criticality), not the one that sorts by CVSS score alone or by age.
5

Risk Scenario Development

A risk scenario is a short, structured story about a plausible event and its effect on business objectives. Scenarios are the bridge between the technical world (threats and vulnerabilities) and the business world (objectives and losses). They are also the unit of analysis in Lesson 4.

Top-downStart from business objectives and ask what events would cause them to fail. Strong at relevance and executive buy-in. May miss technical paths that nobody in the room knows about.
Bottom-upStart from assets, threats, and vulnerabilities and ask what they could lead to. Strong at technical completeness. May produce long lists of scenarios with no clear business link.

Mature programs use both, then reconcile the lists. Top-down scenarios with no technical path are dropped or rewritten. Bottom-up scenarios with no business impact are merged or set aside.

The five components

ComponentMeaningBrightwater example
ActorWho or what causes the event, internal or externalA cybercriminal group
Threat typeThe nature of the event: malicious, accidental, failure, natural, or external requirementMalicious
EventWhat happens: disclosure, interruption, modification, theft, destruction, or ineffective design or executionInterruption
Asset or resourceWhat is affected: information, systems, processes, people, facilitiesThe core banking platform
TimeDuration, timing (when it occurs), and detection lagLasts three days; detected within hours; occurs at month end

Put together: “A cybercriminal group deploys ransomware that interrupts Brightwater’s core banking platform for up to three days during month-end processing. The outage is detected within hours. It threatens the objective of keeping member services available.” That statement can be owned, analyzed, and tested against controls.

6

Scenario Evaluation

A workshop can produce a hundred scenarios in an afternoon. Leadership cannot discuss a hundred. Evaluation turns the raw list into a manageable set that is realistic, relevant, and complete enough to drive decisions.

Tests for every scenario

  • Complete: all five components are present and specific enough to estimate frequency and impact.
  • Plausible: the combination makes sense. A flood can destroy a data center. A flood cannot alter wire instructions.
  • Relevant: it affects a stated business objective and an asset the organization actually has.
  • Realistic: evidence supports it: threat intelligence, incident history, or peer losses. Rare events are kept only when their impact justifies attention.
  • Measurable: duration and detection are defined, so analysts can estimate loss.
  • Distinct: it does not duplicate another scenario in the set.

Reducing the set

  • Consolidate scenarios that share an event and asset into one, noting the different actors as variations.
  • Move from generic to specific: start with generic scenarios such as “loss of a key system,” then specify the few that matter most.
  • Validate with business owners: they confirm that the scenario is realistic and that the impact matters. The practitioner facilitates; the owner validates.
  • Review on a schedule and on change: new threats, new systems, and incidents all trigger a scenario review.
🔎 In practice

Brightwater’s first workshop produced 64 candidate scenarios. Consolidation brought them to 22. Validation with the COO and the head of member services cut them to 12 priority scenarios, including ransomware on the core platform, business email compromise of the wire process, and a misconfigured cloud tenant exposing member data. Those 12 move into the risk register for analysis in Lesson 4.

💡 Exam TipWho should validate that a scenario is realistic and relevant? The business process owner. The risk practitioner facilitates and challenges, but the owner knows the process and will own the risk.

    Faculty Teaching Segment

    Scenario writing is a skill students only learn by doing, and the most useful lesson comes from comparing two methods on the same organization.

    • Signature assignment: split the class into top-down and bottom-up teams, each with a case packet on a fictional credit union. Top-down teams receive the strategic plan and objectives. Bottom-up teams receive an asset list, a synthetic vulnerability scan summary, and two threat advisories. Each team writes eight scenarios using the five components. The teams then meet, reconcile the lists into a single set of ten, and submit a one-page memo explaining what each method found that the other missed.
    • Rubric criteria: every scenario has all five components and a plausible combination; each is tied to a named business objective; the reconciled set is distinct, with duplicates merged and the reasons documented; the memo explains the strengths and blind spots of both methods with specific examples.
    • Lab idea: students take a synthetic list of 25 vulnerabilities with CVSS v4.0 scores, EPSS probabilities, KEV flags, and asset criticality, all supplied by the instructor. They rank the list by CVSS alone, then by a combined rule, and explain the three biggest changes in rank. Use only the synthetic data set you supply.
    • Course fit: this lesson fits an IT risk management, threat intelligence, or vulnerability management course. It supports the CAE-CD Cyber Threats (CTH), Vulnerability Analysis (VLA), and Cybersecurity Planning and Management (CPM) Knowledge Units.
    🎓 Teaching noteRun the Risk Scenario Builder in pairs in Challenge mode. Give each pair a printed scenario card template with the five components, the objective, and the five quality checks. Pairs copy each saved scenario onto a card, then trade cards with another pair, which must find one way to make each scenario more specific. Collect the cards as the input for the Lesson 4 analysis exercise.

    Knowledge Check (self-check)

    Test your understanding

    Select the best answer for each question, then submit for your score and the rationale for every item. Like the exam, several options may be true; choose the best one.

    Q1.A credit union’s scanners report 400 vulnerabilities rated High or Critical by CVSS. The team can fix about 40 this month. What is the BEST way to choose which to fix first?

    Question 1 options
    Correct: C. Risk-based prioritization combines likelihood of exploitation with business impact. CVSS Base scores measure technical severity only, age says nothing about exploitation, and administrator preference is inconsistent and lacks a business view.

    Q2.A risk practitioner is starting a top-down risk scenario exercise. Which input should come FIRST?

    Question 2 options
    Correct: A. Top-down scenarios start from objectives and work toward events. Scan results, indicators, and control test results are bottom-up inputs that help later to confirm technical paths.

    Q3.A draft register entry reads: “Hackers attack our systems.” What is the MOST important improvement?

    Question 3 options
    Correct: D. The entry cannot be analyzed or owned until it is specific. A CVSS score does not define the event, IT is not automatically the risk owner, and inflating a rating without analysis distorts the risk profile.

    Q4.During threat modeling of a wire-transfer system, the team notes that an approver could deny releasing a wire and the logs could not prove otherwise. Which STRIDE category does this represent?

    Question 4 options
    Correct: B. Repudiation is the ability to deny an action without proof to the contrary, addressed by nonrepudiation controls such as signed, protected audit logs. Spoofing is impersonation, tampering is unauthorized change, and elevation of privilege is gaining rights one should not have.

    Q5.What does a CVSS v4.0 Base score PRIMARILY measure?

    Question 5 options
    Correct: D. The Base metrics describe intrinsic technical severity. EPSS estimates the probability of exploitation activity in the next 30 days, the CISA KEV catalog records active exploitation, and business impact comes from the organization’s own context, partly captured by Environmental metrics.

    Q6.A scenario workshop produced 140 candidate risk scenarios. What should the risk practitioner do NEXT?

    Question 6 options
    Correct: B. Scenario evaluation reduces the list to a set that leadership can discuss and analysts can analyze. Analyzing all 140 wastes effort on duplicates, filtering by CVSS ignores non-technical scenarios, and the board needs a prioritized view, not a raw list.

    Q7.A regional credit union wants threat intelligence that reflects attacks on organizations like itself. Which source is MOST useful?

    Question 7 options
    Correct: A. A sector ISAC shares intelligence on campaigns against peer institutions. News is broad and late, brochures are marketing, and the full CVE list covers vulnerabilities, not who is attacking whom.

    Q8.Who should validate that a proposed risk scenario is realistic and relevant to a business process?

    Question 8 options
    Correct: C. The process owner knows how the process works and what failure would cost, and will own the resulting risk. Internal audit must stay independent, a penetration tester sees only technical paths, and a vendor has a conflict of interest.
