Day 2, Morning • Domain 1B

Risk Governance

Organizational governance sets direction. Risk governance decides how much uncertainty the enterprise will carry on the way there, who watches it, and which rules it cannot break. This lesson covers enterprise risk management, the Three Lines Model, the risk profile and risk culture, appetite, tolerance, and capacity, the major risk frameworks, and the legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements that bound every IT risk decision.

Session: Day 2, 9:00 to 12:00 Duration: ~3 hrs Exam Weight: 26% Knowledge Check: 8 Questions

Overview

Set the boundaries before you take the risk

Risk governance answers four questions. How does the enterprise manage risk as a whole? Who does what, from the board to the front line to internal audit? How much risk is acceptable, and how much is too much? Which external obligations limit the choices? IT risk is one part of enterprise risk, so the answers come from the enterprise, not from the IT department.

The CRISC exam tests this domain through ownership and escalation. A practitioner who knows the appetite statement, the tolerance limits, and the reporting lines can tell at once whether a risk needs a decision, an action plan, or nothing more than monitoring.

⚖️ Think like a risk practitionerThe board sets the appetite. Management works within it. The risk practitioner measures risk against it and reports. When a question asks who decides how much risk the enterprise will accept, the answer is senior leadership and the board, not the risk function and not IT.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Explain how ERM frameworks (COSO ERM 2017, ISO 31000:2018, and the ISACA Risk IT Framework) place IT risk inside enterprise risk. Bloom: Understand
  • Assign risk activities to the governing body and to first-line, second-line, and third-line roles under the Three Lines Model. Bloom: Apply
  • Distinguish risk appetite, risk tolerance, and risk capacity, and decide which governance action a given risk level requires. Bloom: Analyze
  • Compare NIST SP 800-37, 800-39, 800-30, CSF 2.0, and ISO/IEC 27005:2022 and select one for a stated need. Bloom: Evaluate
  • Identify the legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements that apply to an IT risk scenario. Bloom: Apply

Enterprise Risk Management

Enterprise risk management treats risk as one portfolio. Credit risk, operational risk, compliance risk, strategic risk, and IT risk are managed with a shared language, shared scales, and one view at the top. The alternative is a set of silos, each with its own heat map, that the board cannot compare or add up.

IT risk is a subset of enterprise risk. A ransomware outage is not only an IT event. It is a failure to deliver services, a possible privacy breach, a financial loss, and a reputational event. ERM makes sure the IT risk register uses the same likelihood and impact scales as the rest of the business, so that IT risks can be ranked against every other risk the enterprise carries.

FrameworkFocusWhat CRISC candidates should know
COSO ERM 2017Enterprise Risk Management: Integrating with Strategy and PerformanceFive components (Governance and Culture; Strategy and Objective-Setting; Performance; Review and Revision; Information, Communication, and Reporting) and 20 principles. Ties risk to strategy and value, not only to loss.
ISO 31000:2018Risk management guidelines for any organizationPrinciples, a framework, and a process: scope, context, and criteria; risk assessment (identification, analysis, evaluation); treatment; monitoring and review; communication and consultation; recording and reporting.
ISACA Risk IT Framework, 2nd ed.IT risk in business terms, aligned with COBIT 2019Connects IT risk to enterprise objectives and appetite. It builds on the original Risk IT domains of risk governance, risk evaluation, and risk response. CRISC thinking follows this framework closely.
COBIT 2019Governance and management of enterprise information and technologyThe governance objective EDM03 (Ensured Risk Optimization) and the management objective APO12 (Managed Risk) place IT risk in the governance system.

What every ERM program needs

  • A mandate from the top: the board approves the ERM policy and the appetite, and senior management owns its execution.
  • A common risk taxonomy: shared categories and definitions, so “high impact” means the same thing in finance and in IT.
  • Common scales and criteria: one set of likelihood and impact definitions, tied to money, people, and service levels.
  • Named owners: every risk on the register has an owner with authority to act on it.
  • A reporting cycle: aggregated risk reaches the board on a schedule, with escalation paths for anything that breaches tolerance in between.

Lines of Defense and the Three Lines Model

The CRISC outline still uses the phrase “lines of defense.” The Institute of Internal Auditors replaced its 2013 “Three Lines of Defense” position paper with the Three Lines Model in 2020. The newer model drops the word “defense” because risk management is about creating and protecting value, not only about blocking loss. It also stresses collaboration among the lines rather than rigid walls between them. Learn both names. Exam items may use either.

RoleWhoResponsibility for IT risk
Governing bodyBoard and its committeesAccountable to stakeholders. Sets appetite, approves the risk policy, oversees management, and ensures internal audit is independent.
First lineOperating management: business units, IT operations, system and process ownersOwns and manages risk day to day. Runs the controls, keeps the risk register current for its area, and acts on findings.
Second lineRisk management, compliance, information security, privacyProvides expertise, frameworks, monitoring, and challenge. Aggregates and reports risk. Does not own the first line’s risks.
Third lineInternal auditIndependent and objective assurance to the governing body on whether governance, risk management, and control work. Reports functionally to the audit committee.
External assuranceExternal auditors, regulators, certification bodiesAdditional assurance outside the three lines, often required by law or contract.
Three Lines of Defense (2013)Three separate lines with a defensive framing. Emphasized segregation between operating management, oversight functions, and audit.
Three Lines Model (2020)Six principles, first-line and second-line “roles” that both sit within management, and a stronger focus on value creation, collaboration, and the governing body’s accountability.

Where the risk practitioner sits

A CRISC holder usually works in a second-line role: building the risk framework, facilitating assessments, challenging the first line’s ratings, and reporting aggregated risk. Some practitioners sit in the first line, embedded in IT or a business unit. Either way, the practitioner advises and the risk owner decides.

🔎 In practice

Larchmont Community Health, a regional clinic network, is moving its electronic health record (EHR) to a cloud provider. The CIO’s team owns migration risk (first line). The enterprise risk office and the privacy officer set assessment criteria and challenge the CIO’s ratings (second line). Internal audit plans a readiness review of the migration controls and reports to the audit committee (third line). The board’s risk committee approves the appetite the program must stay within.

💡 Exam TipIf internal audit is asked to design or run a control, its independence is impaired. When a question asks who should implement a control, the answer is first-line management. When it asks who gives independent assurance, the answer is internal audit.

Risk Profile and Risk Culture

The risk profile is the enterprise’s current risk picture: which risks it carries, how large each one is, and how the total compares with appetite. The profile changes whenever the business changes. A new cloud provider, an acquisition, a new regulation, or a new threat campaign can all move it. A practitioner keeps the profile current and reports when it drifts toward or past tolerance.

What a risk profile contains

  • Current risk levels for each significant risk, usually residual risk after existing controls.
  • Aggregated views by business unit, objective, or risk category, so concentrations become visible.
  • Trend: whether each risk is rising, stable, or falling since the last report.
  • Comparison to appetite and tolerance: which risks are acceptable, which need action, and which need escalation.
  • Key risk indicators that warn when the profile is about to change. Lesson 7 covers KRIs in depth.

Risk culture

Policies describe intended behavior. Culture decides actual behavior. In a healthy risk culture, people report incidents and near misses without fear, managers raise bad news early, and risk is part of ordinary decisions rather than a compliance exercise at year end. In a weak culture, risks are hidden, ratings are softened before reports reach the board, and exceptions become permanent.

Signs of a strong risk cultureLeaders talk about risk in strategy meetings. Near misses are reported and studied. Exceptions have owners and expiry dates. People can name the appetite for their area.
Signs of a weak risk cultureRisk reports are always green. Staff fear blame for reporting problems. Risk is “IT’s job” or “audit’s job.” Exceptions renew automatically every year.
🧭 Culture comes from the topTone at the top sets risk culture, but tone in the middle decides whether staff believe it. Training helps, yet the strongest signal is what happens to the person who reports a problem.
💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks for the BEST indicator of a mature risk culture, prefer an answer about behavior (open reporting, risk discussed in decisions) over one about documents (a published policy, a completed training course).

Risk Appetite, Tolerance, and Capacity

These three terms are related but distinct, and the exam expects you to keep them apart.

ConceptQuestion it answersWho sets itLevel
Risk capacityHow much risk can we absorb before we fail or break our core obligations?A fact about the enterprise: its capital, liquidity, legal limits, and resilience. The board must know it.Enterprise ceiling
Risk appetiteHow much risk are we willing to accept in pursuit of value?The board, with senior managementStrategic and broad
Risk toleranceHow far may performance vary from a specific objective before we must act?Senior management, within the appetiteOperational, per objective or metric

The usual order is appetite < tolerance limits < capacity. Appetite is the target zone. Tolerance is the acceptable variation around objectives, so a risk can sit slightly outside the appetite and still be tolerated while an action plan brings it back. Capacity is the hard ceiling. Setting appetite or tolerance above capacity means the enterprise has agreed to accept risk it cannot survive, which is a governance failure.

Writing appetite and tolerance statements

  • Qualitative appetite: “Larchmont has a low appetite for risks that could compromise patient safety or the confidentiality of health records, and a moderate appetite for technology risk taken on to improve patient access.”
  • Quantitative appetite: “No single IT risk with a residual rating above 9 on the enterprise 5×5 scale may be accepted without risk committee approval.”
  • Tolerance tied to an objective: “EHR availability objective is 99.9% per month. Tolerance: no more than 4 hours of unplanned downtime per month and no single outage longer than 2 hours during clinic hours.”
  • Capacity: stated in terms the enterprise cannot exceed, such as the maximum loss reserves can absorb or the regulatory limit on unavailable patient care services.
🔎 In practice

Larchmont’s board approves a low appetite for privacy risk. During the cloud EHR migration, a data-mapping risk is rated 10, one point above the appetite threshold of 9 but within the tolerance band. The migration director, as risk owner, must submit an action plan with a date. When a second risk, a misconfigured storage bucket holding test data with real patient records, is rated 16, it falls beyond tolerance and is escalated to the risk committee the same week.

💡 Exam TipWhen a residual risk exceeds the appetite, the BEST next step is usually to inform the risk owner and require a response or a formal escalation, not for the practitioner to accept it or to implement a control alone. Appetite decisions belong to the board and senior management.
⚠️ Common mistakeUsing “appetite” and “tolerance” as synonyms. Appetite is the broad, strategic amount of risk the board will accept. Tolerance is the acceptable deviation for a specific objective. One appetite statement can produce many tolerance limits.

Risk Frameworks

Frameworks give structure, shared vocabulary, and evidence that the process is sound. The CRISC exam does not test framework trivia in depth, but it expects you to know what each one is for and to choose the right one for a stated need.

FrameworkPurposeKey structure
NIST SP 800-39Organization-wide information security risk managementThree tiers (organization; mission and business process; information system) and four components (frame, assess, respond, monitor).
NIST SP 800-37 Rev. 2Risk Management Framework for systems and organizationsSeven steps: Prepare, Categorize, Select, Implement, Assess, Authorize, Monitor. Authorization is a senior official’s explicit acceptance of residual risk.
NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1Guide for conducting risk assessmentsPrepare, conduct, communicate, and maintain the assessment. Threat sources, threat events, vulnerabilities, likelihood, and impact.
NIST CSF 2.0 (2024)Cybersecurity outcomes for any organizationSix functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Govern is new in 2.0 and includes risk management strategy, with appetite and tolerance statements.
ISO/IEC 27005:2022Information security risk management guidanceSupports ISO/IEC 27001:2022. Describes event-based and asset-based approaches to risk identification and links to the ISO 31000 process.
ISACA Risk IT 2nd ed. and COBIT 2019IT risk in business termsRisk scenarios, risk ownership, and governance objectives such as EDM03 and APO12.

Choosing a framework

  • Regulatory driver: U.S. federal agencies and many of their contractors follow the NIST RMF. A certifiable management system points to ISO/IEC 27001 with 27005 for risk.
  • Audience: CSF 2.0 outcomes communicate well to boards and partners. SP 800-30 is a practitioner’s method.
  • Existing governance: an enterprise already using COBIT or COSO should align its IT risk method with them rather than start a parallel system.
  • Fit, not purity: most enterprises blend frameworks and map controls across them. What matters is a consistent, documented, repeatable process.
💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks what to do FIRST when adopting a risk framework, look for understanding business objectives, context, and requirements. Picking the framework comes after you know what it must support.

Legal, Regulatory, and Contractual Requirements

External obligations narrow the choices. Some risks cannot be accepted, no matter what the appetite says, because a law requires a control. The practitioner’s job is to know which obligations apply, map them to controls and owners, and report compliance risk in the same register as other risks.

RequirementApplies toIT risk relevance
SOX (Sarbanes-Oxley Act, 2002)U.S. public companiesSection 404 requires management to assess internal control over financial reporting, including IT general controls over financial systems.
GLBAU.S. financial institutionsRequires safeguards for customer financial information, with regulator-specific rules such as the FTC Safeguards Rule.
HIPAAU.S. covered entities and business associatesThe Security Rule requires risk analysis and safeguards for electronic protected health information, and business associate agreements with vendors.
GDPRProcessing of personal data of people in the EUSecurity appropriate to the risk, data protection impact assessments for high-risk processing, and breach notification to the supervisory authority within 72 hours where required.
PCI DSS v4.0Entities that store, process, or transmit payment card dataA contractual standard enforced through card brands and acquirers. Version 4.0 added a targeted risk analysis for some requirements.
SEC cybersecurity disclosure rules (2023)U.S. public companiesDisclose material cybersecurity incidents on Form 8-K within four business days of determining materiality, and describe cyber risk management, strategy, and governance in annual reports.
Contracts and SLAsAny enterprise with customers or vendorsAvailability commitments, security clauses, right-to-audit terms, breach notification duties, and liability caps.

Managing compliance risk

  • Maintain an obligations register: each law, regulation, standard, and key contract, mapped to controls and named owners.
  • Watch for change: legal and compliance teams track new rules. The risk practitioner assesses what each change does to the risk profile.
  • Read contracts as risk documents: an SLA with a 99.5% availability commitment sets a tolerance the enterprise must plan for, and a vendor contract without audit rights limits assurance.
  • Treat conflicts openly: when obligations conflict, such as data residency in one country and a provider’s global support model, document the decision and its owner.

Set the board’s appetite threshold, the width of the tolerance band above it, and the enterprise’s capacity.     🔎 In practice

    The heat map turns an appetite statement into a routing rule. Green risks are accepted and monitored by their owners. Amber risks stay with the owner but need a dated action plan. Red risks leave the owner’s desk and go to senior management for a decision. Anything past capacity goes to the board. A practitioner who can show this map at the risk committee turns a debate about scores into a decision about actions.

    💡 Exam TipThe widget teaches the exam’s core escalation rule: a risk above appetite is not automatically a crisis, and a risk above tolerance is never the practitioner’s to accept. Real tolerance is set per objective, not as one band on a heat map, so read the band as a simplification of many objective-level limits.

    Faculty Teaching Segment

    Students often learn appetite as a definition and never write one. A short workshop where they draft, test, and defend an appetite statement makes the difference between appetite, tolerance, and capacity concrete.

    • Signature assignment: teams receive a two-page profile of a fictional community college (enrollment, budget, strategic plan, a planned move of the student information system to a cloud provider). They draft a qualitative appetite statement for three risk categories (student data privacy, service availability, and innovation in online learning), convert each into a quantitative threshold on a 5×5 scale, write two tolerance limits tied to specific objectives, and state the college’s capacity in one sentence. They then place six supplied risks on a heat map and name the required governance action for each.
    • Rubric criteria: appetite statements are clearly owned by the board and linked to strategic objectives; tolerance limits are measurable and tied to a named objective; capacity is stated separately and sits above tolerance; each risk is routed to the correct action and owner with a one-line justification.
    • Lab idea: give students a synthetic risk register of 15 risks in a spreadsheet. They build a conditional-format heat map driven by appetite and tolerance cells, then change the board stance and record how many risks change route. Use only the synthetic register you supply.
    • Course fit: this lesson fits an IT risk management, GRC, or security management course. It supports the CAE-CD Cybersecurity Planning and Management (CPM), Policy, Legal, Ethics, and Compliance (PLE), and IA Compliance (IAC) Knowledge Units.
    🎓 Teaching noteRun the Appetite and Tolerance Map in pairs. Give each pair a printed worksheet with the eight Larchmont risks and three board stances. Pairs record the action for each risk under each stance and then write one sentence explaining why capacity did not move. Collect the worksheets as an exit ticket.

    Knowledge Check (self-check)

    Test your understanding

    Select the best answer for each question, then submit for your score and the rationale for every item. Like the exam, several options may be true; choose the best one.

    Q1.A clinic network is drafting its first enterprise risk appetite statement. Who should approve it?

    Question 1 options
    Correct: B. Appetite is a strategic decision that the board sets with senior management. The CISO and the ERM function help develop and apply it but do not own it. Internal audit would lose independence if it approved the appetite it later audits against.

    Q2.A residual IT risk is rated above the enterprise risk appetite but inside the approved tolerance. What should the risk practitioner do NEXT?

    Question 2 options
    Correct: D. Inside tolerance but above appetite means the owner must act, but the risk does not need board escalation. The practitioner never accepts risk for the business, and implementing controls is a first-line task chosen by the owner.

    Q3.Under the IIA Three Lines Model, which function provides independent assurance to the governing body on the effectiveness of IT risk management?

    Question 3 options
    Correct: A. Internal audit is the third line and gives independent, objective assurance. Risk management and information security are second-line roles that monitor and challenge but sit within management. IT operations is first line and owns the risk.

    Q4.An enterprise’s reserves could absorb a single loss of about US$20 million. The board has stated it will accept losses up to US$5 million from any one technology initiative. What does the US$20 million figure represent?

    Question 4 options
    Correct: C. Capacity is the most risk the enterprise can absorb. The US$5 million figure is the appetite the board chose, which sits well below capacity. Tolerance is the acceptable variation around a specific objective, and residual risk is the risk that remains after controls.

    Q5.A team wants a framework that defines organization-wide risk management across three tiers: organization, mission and business process, and information system. Which source BEST fits?

    Question 5 options
    Correct: A. SP 800-39 defines the three-tier approach and the frame, assess, respond, monitor components. SP 800-53 and ISO/IEC 27002 are control catalogs, and PCI DSS is a contractual standard for card data.

    Q6.Risk reports at a company have shown every IT risk as green for three years, yet two significant incidents occurred. What is the MOST likely underlying issue?

    Question 6 options
    Correct: D. Reports that are always green while incidents occur suggest risks are being hidden or understated, a culture problem. A low appetite would make more risks red, not fewer. Adopting a framework or changing colors would not fix behavior.

    Q7.A business unit proposes accepting a gap in encryption of payment card data because the residual risk rating falls within appetite. What is the BEST response from the risk practitioner?

    Question 7 options
    Correct: B. Contractual and regulatory obligations constrain appetite. PCI DSS requires protection of stored cardholder data, and only a documented compensating control can stand in. The practitioner does not approve acceptance, audit should not approve exceptions it will review, and insurance does not satisfy the requirement.

    Q8.What is the PRIMARY reason to integrate the IT risk register into the enterprise risk management program?

    Question 8 options
    Correct: C. ERM’s main value is a single, comparable view of risk tied to objectives and appetite. Tool savings and audit documentation are side benefits. Ownership stays with the business, not with the ERM function.
