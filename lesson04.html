Day 3, Morning • Domain 2B

Risk Analysis

A risk scenario says what could happen. Risk analysis says how often, how badly, and whether the enterprise can live with it. This lesson moves from business impact analysis and the risk register to qualitative, semi-quantitative, and quantitative methods, ending with a FAIR-style Monte Carlo model that turns honest ranges into a loss exceedance curve a board can read.

Session: Day 3, 9:00 to 12:30 Duration: ~3.5 hrs Exam Weight: 22% Knowledge Check: 8 Questions

Overview

From “it could happen” to “how much, how often”

Risk identification produced a set of realistic scenarios. Analysis gives each one a size. It estimates likelihood and impact, compares the result with the enterprise’s appetite and tolerance, and records it in a risk register where an owner can act on it. The method can be a color on a heat map or a dollar distribution from ten thousand simulated years. What matters is that the method fits the decision being made.

This lesson covers the assessment standards, business impact analysis, the risk register, the main analysis methodologies (including SLE and ALE, FAIR, Monte Carlo, and bow-tie analysis), and the difference between inherent, residual, and current risk. The flagship interactive lets you build a loss exceedance curve and test it against a stated tolerance.

⚖️ Think like a risk practitionerAnalysis serves a decision. The risk practitioner estimates and explains; the risk owner decides. A precise number nobody can act on is worth less than an honest range tied to the enterprise’s tolerance. When a question offers a technical score and a business-impact answer, the business-impact answer is usually the better one.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Explain how NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1, ISO 31000:2018, and ISO/IEC 27005:2022 structure a risk assessment. Bloom: Understand

  • Specify the fields of a useful risk register and link each entry to an owner, KRIs, and an action plan. Bloom: Apply
  • Compare qualitative, semi-quantitative, and quantitative methods, and calculate SLE and ALE. Bloom: Analyze

Risk Analysis Concepts and Standards

Risk is a combination of two things: how likely an adverse event is, and how much harm it would cause. Likelihood can be expressed as a probability, a frequency per year, or an ordinal rating. Impact can be expressed in money, in downtime, in records exposed, or in a rating tied to a business objective. Analysis estimates both for each scenario, then evaluation compares the result with criteria the enterprise has already set, such as appetite and tolerance.

The CRISC exam does not require you to memorize any one standard, but it expects you to recognize their shared logic. Each standard puts context and criteria first, then identification, analysis, and evaluation, then treatment, with communication and monitoring running throughout.

StandardWhat it contributes to analysisKey structure
NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1A detailed guide for conducting information security risk assessments, with threat sources, threat events, vulnerabilities, predisposing conditions, likelihood, and impact.Prepare, conduct, communicate, maintain. Conduct runs from threat sources to likelihood, impact, and risk.
ISO 31000:2018Enterprise-wide principles and process for any kind of risk. Defines risk as the effect of uncertainty on objectives.Scope, context, and criteria; assessment (identification, analysis, evaluation); treatment; monitoring and review; recording and reporting.
ISO/IEC 27005:2022Information security risk management guidance aligned to ISO/IEC 27001:2022. Supports both event-based and asset-based identification.Context, assessment, treatment, acceptance, communication, monitoring and review.
ISACA Risk IT Framework, 2nd ed.Connects IT risk to enterprise risk and business objectives, with scenario-based analysis and risk aggregation.Risk governance, risk evaluation, risk response.
FAIR (Open Group O-RT, O-RA)A quantitative taxonomy that decomposes risk into loss event frequency and loss magnitude, measured in money.Risk = LEF × LM, with ranges and simulation.

Good analysis has four qualities

  • Relevant: it measures impact on business objectives, not on servers. “Members cannot send wires for two days” is relevant. “Server 14 is down” is not, until it is translated.
  • Consistent: the same scales, criteria, and assumptions are used across the enterprise, so two risks can be compared and aggregated.
  • Transparent: sources, assumptions, and the confidence in each estimate are written down, so a reviewer can challenge them.
  • Proportionate: the effort matches the decision. A small, reversible decision does not need a two-week quantitative study.
💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks what the risk practitioner should do FIRST before analyzing a risk, look for an answer about understanding the business context or the criteria (appetite, tolerance, impact scales). Analysis without agreed criteria cannot be evaluated.

Business Impact Analysis

A business impact analysis (BIA) asks a simple question with a complicated answer: if this process stops, what happens to the enterprise, and how fast does it get worse? The BIA is a business exercise. IT supplies the dependency map, but process owners supply the impact, because only they know what a lost day costs in members, money, regulatory standing, and trust.

The BIA sequence

  • Identify critical processes: start from business objectives and list the processes that deliver them. Not every process is critical, and treating them all as critical makes none of them a priority.
  • Assess impact over time: estimate financial, operational, legal, and reputational impact at intervals (one hour, one day, one week). Impact usually grows nonlinearly.
  • Set recovery objectives: the business owner sets the MTD. The RTO and RPO follow from it.
  • Map dependencies: applications, infrastructure, data, people, facilities, and third parties. A process is only as recoverable as its slowest dependency.
  • Validate and approve: senior management confirms priorities, because recovery capacity is funded from them.
Brightwater processMTDRTORPOKey dependencies
Outbound wire transfers8 hours4 hours15 minutesCore banking, payment network gateway, dual-control approvers
Online and mobile banking24 hours12 hours1 hourDigital banking vendor, identity service, core banking
Loan origination3 days48 hours24 hoursLoan platform (SaaS), credit bureau feeds, document storage
Marketing campaigns2 weeks7 days1 weekEmail platform, member list export

The figures above are illustrative values for the fictional credit union, chosen to show the relationships. The RTO sits inside the MTD with room to spare, because restoring a system is not the same as resuming the business. Staff must verify data, catch up on backlog, and reconcile transactions before the process is truly back.

RTO: how fastTime to restore the process or system. It drives the recovery strategy: hot site, warm site, cloud failover, or manual workaround.
RPO: how much dataThe point in time to which data must be recoverable. It drives backup frequency, journaling, and replication.
🔎 In practice

Brightwater’s IT team proposed nightly backups for the wire system. The BIA showed that wire instructions received after the last backup could not be reconstructed, and the treasury manager set a 15-minute RPO. The gap between “nightly” and “15 minutes” became a risk register entry with a named owner, not an IT preference.

💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks who should determine recovery time or tolerable downtime, choose the business process owner, supported by the BIA. IT recommends strategies to meet the objective; it does not set the objective.
3

The Risk Register

The risk register is the working record of the risk program. It is where analysis results live, where owners are named, and where responses and indicators are tracked. A register that lists threats with no owners, dates, or links to action is an inventory, not a management tool.

FieldWhy it matters
Risk ID and titleA stable reference for reports, action plans, and audit trails.
Scenario statementActor, event, asset or process, and consequence, written so a business reader understands it.
Business objective affectedLinks the risk to strategy and makes aggregation by objective possible.
Risk ownerThe person accountable for the response decision and for accepting residual risk.
Inherent ratingLikelihood and impact before responses, using the enterprise scale or a dollar range.
Existing controls and ownersWhat is in place today and who operates it, with the latest test result.
Current and residual ratingThe level now, given actual control performance, and the level expected after planned responses.
Response and action planThe option chosen, the tasks, the owner of each task, the due date, and the status.
KRIsThe indicators that warn when the risk is moving toward tolerance, with thresholds.
Review date and historyWhen it was last assessed, by whom, and what changed.

Registers exist at more than one level. A business unit register holds detailed IT risks. The enterprise register holds aggregated risks that matter to senior management and the board. The two must be linked, so that several small entries with a common cause can be seen as one larger exposure.

📌 Register hygieneDuplicate entries, stale ratings, and risks owned by “IT” rather than a named person are the three most common register defects. Each one quietly breaks reporting and aggregation.
💡 Exam TipIf a question asks for the PRIMARY benefit of a well-maintained risk register, prefer answers about accountability and informed decisions (owners, responses, and status in one place) over answers about satisfying auditors.
4

Risk Analysis Methodologies

There is no single correct methodology. There is a method that fits the decision, the data available, and the audience. The CRISC exam expects you to know what each method does well, where it breaks, and how to move from one to another.

MethodOutputStrengthsWeaknesses
QualitativeRatings such as Low, Medium, High; heat mapsFast, inclusive, good for screening many risksSubjective; ratings mean different things to different people; cannot support a cost-benefit decision
Semi-quantitativeNumeric scores (for example, 1 to 5 times 1 to 5)Ranks risks; easy to aggregate in a spreadsheetThe numbers are ordinal labels, so multiplying them can mislead; a “20” is not twice a “10”
QuantitativeDollars per year, probabilities, distributionsSupports cost-benefit, insurance, and capital decisions; comparable across risksNeeds data and calibrated estimates; can look more precise than it is

Classic quantitative: SLE and ALE

  • Asset value (AV): the value of the asset or process at risk.
  • Exposure factor (EF): the fraction of that value lost in one event.
  • Single loss expectancy (SLE): SLE = AV × EF.
  • Annualized rate of occurrence (ARO): expected events per year. Once every five years is 0.2.
  • Annualized loss expectancy (ALE): ALE = SLE × ARO. A control is cost-justified when the ALE it removes exceeds its annual cost.

Example: a $2,000,000 payment platform, an exposure factor of 25%, and an event expected once every five years. SLE = $500,000; ALE = $500,000 × 0.2 = $100,000 per year. The weakness is that every input is a single point. Nobody knows the exposure factor is exactly 25%, and the formula hides that uncertainty.

FAIR: decomposing risk into ranges

Factor Analysis of Information Risk (FAIR), published by The Open Group as the Risk Taxonomy (O-RT) and Risk Analysis (O-RA) standards, measures risk in money and breaks it into estimable parts.

  • Loss event frequency (LEF): threat event frequency (how often a threat acts against the asset) times vulnerability (the probability that a threat event becomes a loss event).
  • Loss magnitude (LM): primary loss to the organization plus secondary loss from the reactions of external stakeholders such as regulators, customers, and litigants.
  • Six forms of loss: productivity, response, replacement, fines and judgments, competitive advantage, and reputation.
  • Ranges, not points: each factor is estimated as a minimum, most likely, and maximum, often by calibrated subject-matter experts, and fitted to a PERT distribution.

Monte Carlo simulation

Monte Carlo simulation samples each uncertain input thousands of times and records the result of each trial. In a FAIR-style model, each trial is one simulated year: draw a frequency, draw a number of events, draw a loss for each event, and add them up. Sorting thousands of simulated years produces a distribution of annual loss. From it you can read the mean (the ALE), percentiles such as P90, and the probability of exceeding any amount. Plotting that probability against loss gives the loss exceedance curve.

Bow-tie analysis

A bow-tie diagram places the risk event (the “knot”) in the center. Causes and threats sit on the left, with preventive controls between them and the event. Consequences sit on the right, with detective, corrective, and recovery controls between the event and each consequence. It is a qualitative tool, but it is excellent for showing an owner which controls reduce frequency and which reduce magnitude, which is exactly the split FAIR uses.

🔎 In practice

Brightwater’s ransomware scenario rated “High” on the heat map for three years in a row. The board asked a different question: how much should we budget for it? A FAIR-style analysis estimated an event frequency of 0.1 to 1.0 per year (most likely 0.3) and a loss per event of $150,000 to $4,000,000 (most likely $600,000). The simulation produced a mean annual loss above $400,000 and a 17% chance of losing more than $1,000,000 in a year. Those two numbers started a budget conversation the heat map never did.

💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks which method BEST supports a cost-benefit decision about a control, choose quantitative analysis. When it asks which method is BEST for quickly prioritizing a long list of risks with limited data, choose qualitative. Neither is “better” in the abstract.

Inherent, Residual, and Current Risk; Aggregation

One scenario can carry three ratings at once, and confusing them is a common exam trap.

RatingQuestion it answersTypical use
Inherent riskHow big is this risk with no controls or responses in place?Shows how much the enterprise depends on its controls; sets the priority for control assurance.
Current riskHow big is it today, given how the controls are actually performing?Reflects test results, open findings, and failed KCIs. It can be worse than planned residual risk.
Residual riskHow big will it be after the selected responses are fully in place and working?Compared with appetite and tolerance; accepted formally by the risk owner.

The difference between inherent and residual risk is the work the controls are expected to do. A large gap means a large dependency. If those controls fail, the enterprise is back at inherent risk without warning, which is why high-dependency controls deserve independent testing and key control indicators.

Risk aggregation

  • Why aggregate: several risks that are each within tolerance can combine into an exposure that is not. Senior management needs the combined view.
  • Common causes: risks that share a cause (an unsupported platform, a single critical vendor, a shared identity service) can occur together, so their losses are correlated.
  • How to aggregate: roll up by business objective, by cause, or by asset class. With quantitative data, simulate the risks together so correlations are captured; simply adding averages hides the tail.
  • Risk profile: the aggregated view, at a point in time, of the risks the enterprise faces. It is what senior management compares with appetite.
⚠️ Common mistakeReporting planned residual risk as if it were current risk. If the planned control is still being implemented, or failed its last test, the current risk is closer to inherent. Report what is true today and show the plan separately.
💡 Exam TipWhen several business units report low-rated risks that share a common cause, the BEST next step is usually to aggregate them and reassess at the enterprise level, not to accept each one separately.

Interactive: Loss Exceedance Simulator

This simulator runs a FAIR-style model of a Brightwater Credit Union scenario. Enter a minimum, most likely, and maximum for loss event frequency (events per year) and for loss magnitude (dollars per event). For each of 5,000 simulated years, the model draws an event rate from a PERT distribution, draws a Poisson count of events at that rate, draws a PERT loss for each event, and adds them up. The control effect slider removes that share of events (a frequency control), so you see the inherent curve and the residual curve together. The red zone is the tolerance statement: annual loss above the threshold should happen no more often than the stated probability. The random number generator is seeded, so the same inputs always give the same result.

🔎 In practice

With the default ransomware inputs and a control that prevents 30% of events, the residual curve still crosses the $1M threshold above the 10% line. Brightwater’s chief operating officer, the risk owner, did not need a lecture on Monte Carlo. She needed one sentence: “There is about a one-in-eight chance this costs us more than $1 million in a given year, and you told us one-in-ten is the most you will accept.” Press “New random seed” a few times: the answer moves by about a point at most, which shows that 5,000 trials are enough for a decision and that the uncertainty that matters is in the input ranges, not the sampling. It also shows why a result within a point of the limit is reported as too close to call.

💡 Exam TipThis widget teaches three exam lessons. Risk is evaluated against tolerance, not against a single expected value. The gap between inherent and residual risk is the dependency on controls, so those controls need assurance. And a single-point ALE understates skewed losses, which is why the BEST quantitative answer usually uses ranges and reports the probability of exceeding a threshold.

Faculty Teaching Segment

Students often believe quantitative analysis needs special software or perfect data. Rebuilding this simulator in a spreadsheet shows them that it needs neither, only honest ranges and a clear question.

  • Signature assignment: give teams a one-page scenario packet for a fictional regional employer (a ransomware event on a scheduling system, with synthetic incident history and cost data). Teams write a FAIR-style analysis: scenario statement, calibrated ranges for frequency and loss with the source of each estimate, a 1,000-row Monte Carlo in a spreadsheet, a loss exceedance chart, and a one-page memo to the risk owner that compares the result with a stated tolerance.
  • Rubric criteria: (1) ranges are justified by stated evidence or expert reasoning, not chosen for convenience; (2) the model is mechanically correct, with frequency and magnitude sampled separately and summed per simulated year; (3) the memo evaluates the result against tolerance and names the decision the owner must make; (4) limitations and the single-point comparison are explained in plain business language.
  • Lab idea: students build the model in Excel or Google Sheets. Each row is a year. A frequency cell uses RAND() with an inverse distribution (a triangular approximation of PERT is acceptable for a first course), a Poisson count via POISSON.DIST lookups or a simple Bernoulli for rare events, and a loss per event from BETA.INV(RAND(), alpha, beta, min, max) with the PERT shape formulas. A COUNTIF over a column of thresholds produces the exceedance curve. Use synthetic data only.
  • Course fit: an IT risk management, GRC, or business continuity course at the upper-division or graduate level. Supports the CAE-CD Cybersecurity Planning and Management (CPM), Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery (BCD), and IA Compliance (IAC) Knowledge Units.
🎓 Teaching noteRun the simulator in pairs. One student is the risk practitioner and adjusts only the ranges; the other is the risk owner and adjusts only the control effect and tolerance. Each pair submits a one-page worksheet (provided as a file) that records the inputs, the four tiles, and a two-sentence recommendation. Then ask every pair to narrow the loss range by half and report how much the verdict changed. Students discover that better estimates can change a decision as much as a new control.

Knowledge Check (self-check)

Test your understanding

Select the best answer for each question, then submit for your score and the rationale for every item. Like the exam, several options may be true; choose the best one.

Q1.During a business impact analysis, Brightwater’s treasury manager states that wire instructions received in the last 15 minutes before an outage could not be reconstructed and must not be lost. Which recovery objective does this statement set?

Question 1 options
Correct: B. The RPO is the maximum data loss, measured in time, the business can accept, and it drives backup and replication frequency. RTO and MTD describe how long the process can be down, not how much data can be lost. A gateway service level is a vendor commitment, not a BIA objective.

Q2.The board has seen a ransomware risk rated “High” on the heat map for three years and now asks how much it should budget for the exposure. Which approach BEST answers the board’s question?

Question 2 options
Correct: D. A budget question needs a monetary answer, and ranges with simulation produce both the expected loss and the probability of large losses. A finer matrix and a multiplied ordinal score are still not dollars. CVSS rates vulnerability severity, not business loss.

Q3.A risk register lists threats and ratings but no names in the “owner” column; every entry says “IT.” What is the PRIMARY concern with this register?

Question 3 options
Correct: A. Risk ownership creates accountability for the response and for accepting what remains. A department is not an owner. An audit finding and workload are symptoms of that gap, and tool compatibility is a minor technical issue.

Q4.A risk was rated Low because a backup control reduces its impact. The latest restore test failed, and the fix will take three months. What should the risk practitioner do FIRST?

Question 4 options
Correct: C. Current risk must reflect how controls actually perform, so the rating is out of date and the owner needs to know. Keeping the planned rating misreports the exposure. Redesigning the process belongs to the control owner. Recommending acceptance comes after the risk is correctly assessed, not before.

Q5.A Monte Carlo analysis shows a mean annual loss of $280,000 and a 12% chance of losing more than $1 million in a year. The enterprise tolerance is no more than a 10% chance of losing over $1 million. The single-point estimate is $126,000. What should the risk practitioner report to the risk owner?

Question 5 options
Correct: A. Tolerance is stated as a probability of exceeding a loss, so the exceedance probability is the measure to compare. The single point ignores the skewed tail, and the mean says nothing about how often large losses occur. Rerunning a simulation to get a preferred answer is not analysis.

Q6.Three business units each report a Low risk related to the same end-of-support database platform. Each unit plans to accept its risk. What is the BEST next step for the risk practitioner?

Question 6 options
Correct: D. Risks with a common cause can occur together, so their combined exposure may exceed tolerance even when each is Low. The practitioner does not approve acceptances, changing ratings without analysis is arbitrary, and scheduling an upgrade is a response decision that comes after the combined risk is understood.

Q7.A payment platform is valued at $2,000,000. A specific outage scenario would cost 25% of that value per occurrence and is expected once every five years. What is the annualized loss expectancy?

Question 7 options
Correct: B. SLE = $2,000,000 × 0.25 = $500,000. ARO = 1 / 5 = 0.2. ALE = $500,000 × 0.2 = $100,000. Option A is the SLE, not the ALE. Option C applies the ARO to the full asset value instead of the SLE, and D multiplies instead of dividing by the interval.

Q8.Who should determine the maximum tolerable downtime for Brightwater’s loan origination process?

Question 8 options
Correct: C. Only the business owner can judge when downtime becomes unacceptable to the business, so the owner sets the MTD using BIA evidence. IT designs a recovery strategy to meet it, the practitioner facilitates and advises, and a vendor SLA is a dependency to compare against the MTD, not the source of it.
