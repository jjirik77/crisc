Day 1, Morning • All Domains

The CRISC Exam and the Risk Practitioner Mindset

CRISC does not ask what a security engineer would build. It asks what a risk practitioner would advise, and who should decide. This orientation explains how the exam is built and scored, who qualifies, what it costs to earn and keep, and the professional lens that turns plausible answers into the best answer.

Session: Day 1, 9:00 to 10:30 Duration: ~1.5 hrs Exam Weight: All domains Knowledge Check: 8 Questions

Overview

Advise the owner, then let the owner decide

Certified in Risk and Information Systems Control (CRISC) is ISACA’s credential for professionals who identify, assess, respond to, and report on IT risk, and who design and monitor the controls that keep it within appetite. The exam is practitioner-level. Most questions describe a realistic situation and ask for the FIRST, BEST, or MOST appropriate action. Several options are usually true. Only one is what an experienced risk practitioner would do next.

This lesson sets the frame for the week. It covers the exam format and the current domain weights, eligibility and maintenance, the ISACA Code of Professional Ethics, the 24 supporting tasks, the best-answer tactics that the rest of the workshop practices, and how CRISC differs from CISA, CISM, and CISSP. It ends with an interactive that tests the single most important CRISC habit: knowing who owns each decision.

⚖️ Think like a risk practitionerThe practitioner identifies, analyzes, recommends, facilitates, and reports. The risk owner decides how to respond and accepts what remains. Senior management and the board set appetite and accept risk beyond it. When an answer has the practitioner making the owner’s decision, it is almost always wrong.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Describe the CRISC exam format, scoring, four domains and their weights, and what changed in the current outline. Bloom: Understand
  • Explain the eligibility, application, CPE, and fee requirements for earning and keeping the credential. Bloom: Understand
  • Apply the ISACA Code of Professional Ethics to situations a risk practitioner faces. Bloom: Apply
  • Assign common risk decisions to the correct owner: board, risk owner, control owner, risk practitioner, or internal audit. Bloom: Analyze
  • Use best-answer tactics (FIRST, BEST, MOST, PRIMARY) to eliminate plausible but weaker options. Bloom: Evaluate

The Exam at a Glance

The CRISC exam has 150 multiple-choice questions across four domains, delivered in 4 hours. Results are reported as a scaled score from 200 to 800, and 450 passes. The time limit and scoring follow ISACA’s standard exam policy, so confirm them, along with scheduling, identification, and retake rules, in the current ISACA Exam Candidate Guide before you register.

Four hours for 150 questions is about 1.6 minutes per question. That is enough time to read each scenario twice, which you should. Most wrong answers on CRISC come from misreading the role in the question or the step in the process, not from missing a fact.

DomainWeightPartsWorkshop lessons
1. Governance26%A. Organizational Governance; B. Risk GovernanceL1, L2
2. Risk Assessment22%A. Risk Identification; B. Risk AnalysisL3, L4
3. Risk Response and Reporting32%A. Risk Response; B. Control Design and Implementation; C. Risk Monitoring and ReportingL5, L6, L7
4. Technology and Security20%A. Technology and Security; B. Information Security PrinciplesL8, L9

At 32%, Domain 3 is the largest single block, about 48 of 150 questions. Together, Domains 1 and 3 make up more than half of the exam. Both are about decisions and accountability, which is why ownership is the theme of this first lesson.

What changed in the current outline

The outline in effect since November 2025 kept the four-domain structure but made three visible changes.

  • Domain 2 renamed: IT Risk Assessment became Risk Assessment. The scope is enterprise risk that involves technology, not only IT department risk.
  • Domain 4 renamed: Information Technology and Security became Technology and Security.
  • Weights shifted: 2 points moved from Domain 4 to Domain 2. Risk Assessment rose from 20% to 22%, and Technology and Security fell from 22% to 20%.
  • Emerging technology and AI: ISACA’s CRISC page highlights AI risk assessment, AI data governance, and ethics. In the outline, this shows up under emerging technologies (Domain 4A), emerging risks (Domain 3C), and the supporting task on evaluating emerging technologies.
💡 Exam TipOlder study guides, practice banks, and blog posts still use the earlier names and weights. The concepts largely carry over, but check every resource against the current outline at isaca.org, and budget your study time by the current weights.
🔎 In practice

A faculty member with a strong audit background plans to spend most of her study time on control testing. Mapping her plan to the weights shows that control design and testing is one part of Domain 3, while governance and risk response together carry far more questions. She rebalances toward ownership, appetite, and response selection.

Eligibility, Application, CPE, and Fees

Passing the exam is one requirement. Certification also requires verified work experience, an application, agreement to the Code of Professional Ethics, and ongoing CPE.

Experience and application

  • Experience: at least 3 years of CRISC work experience, covering at least 2 of the 4 domains, gained within the 10 years before the application date.
  • Sequence: candidates may sit for the exam first. After passing, they must apply for certification within 5 years.
  • Waivers: ISACA lists no experience waivers or substitutions for CRISC. A degree or another credential does not replace the 3 years.
  • Verification: the application includes experience that a supervisor or another qualified party verifies. Faculty who teach risk and also consult, serve on governance committees, or run risk assessments should document that work carefully.

Costs and maintenance

ItemISACA memberNon-member
Exam registrationUS$575US$760
Certification applicationUS$50 (after passing)
Annual maintenance feeUS$45US$85
CPEAt least 20 hours a year and 120 hours per 3-year cycle

Both CPE minimums apply. A holder who earns 60 hours in one year and none in the next has met neither the annual floor nor the spirit of the rule. ISACA’s CPE policy allows audits of reported hours, so keep certificates, agendas, or other evidence. Teaching a risk course and developing new course material can qualify, subject to the policy’s limits; check the current CPE policy for what counts.

💰 Budget noteFor a department sending several faculty, membership often pays for itself through the exam discount alone. Fees and policies change, so treat these figures as current at the time of writing and confirm them on isaca.org before you budget.
💡 Exam TipEligibility details are not exam content. Do not spend study time memorizing fees. Spend it on the 24 tasks and on who decides what.

The ISACA Code of Professional Ethics and the Practitioner’s Role

Every ISACA credential holder agrees to the ISACA Code of Professional Ethics. Violations can lead to an investigation and disciplinary action, up to loss of the credential. Read the current text at isaca.org. The table below paraphrases its themes and shows how each one plays out in risk work.

Theme of the Code (paraphrased)What it looks like in risk work
Support effective governance and standardsEncourage adoption of recognized frameworks and controls rather than ad hoc practice.
Objectivity, due diligence, professional careBase ratings on evidence, not on who sponsors the project. Disclose conflicts of interest.
Serve stakeholders lawfully and with integrityDo not help a business unit hide a risk to meet a deadline.
Privacy and confidentialityUse incident and assessment data only for its purpose; protect it like the asset it is.
Maintain competenceTake on only work you can do well; keep current on threats, technology, and AI.
Report results fullyTell the appropriate parties what you found, including significant facts that are unwelcome.
Support professional educationHelp stakeholders understand risk and controls. Faculty do this every term.

The role behind the ethics

The Code explains why ownership matters so much on this exam. A practitioner who quietly accepts a risk on behalf of a manager has done two things wrong: taken a decision that belongs to someone else, and kept a significant fact from the person accountable for it. The honest path and the correct governance path are the same: analyze, recommend, document, and escalate to the person with authority.

The practitioner doesIdentifies and analyzes risk, develops scenarios, maintains the register, recommends responses, facilitates appetite discussions, monitors KRIs, and reports to stakeholders.
The practitioner does notAccept risk on behalf of the business, set appetite, own the business process, or audit its own work. Independence belongs to internal audit.

🔎 In practice

At Penmore Technical College, a dean asks the risk analyst to mark an overdue access review as “in progress” so the board report looks clean. The analyst reports it as overdue, adds the dean’s remediation date and reason, and lets the board see both. Objectivity and full reporting are kept, and the dean still gets credit for a plan.

The 24 Supporting Tasks

The current outline lists 24 supporting tasks: the activities a risk practitioner actually performs. The domains describe what you must know. The tasks describe what you must do. Exam items test both, usually by putting you inside a task and asking for the next step.

The table below paraphrases the tasks and groups them by the domain where this workshop teaches them. ISACA presents the tasks as one numbered list that supports all four domains, so use the official outline for exact wording and numbering.

ThemeTasks, paraphrasedLessons
Context and governance (D1)Gather and evaluate information about the business and its technology environment; align risk work with strategy and objectives; help stakeholders set appetite and tolerance; promote a risk-aware culture and training; support policies and frameworks for managing risk.L1, L2
Identification and analysis (D2)Identify threats, vulnerabilities, and risk events; build and evaluate risk scenarios; analyze likelihood and impact; establish and maintain the risk register; assess inherent and residual risk and the effect on objectives.L3, L4
Response and ownership (D3A)Recommend response options to risk owners; establish risk and control ownership; manage third-party and supply chain risk; track issues, findings, and exceptions; develop and follow risk action plans.L5
Controls (D3B)Help design, select, and implement controls; assess control design and operating effectiveness; recommend control improvements.L6
Monitoring and reporting (D3C)Define and monitor KRIs, KCIs, and KPIs; collect, aggregate, and validate risk data; report risk to stakeholders in terms they can act on; scan for emerging risks.L7
Technology and security (D4)Assess risk across technology operations, projects, the SDLC, and the data lifecycle; support resilience and recovery planning; apply security and privacy principles; evaluate emerging technologies and their risk (Task 22).L8, L9
Exercises (all domains)Facilitate tabletop exercises that test plans, roles, and decisions under realistic pressure (Task 24).L9

Two tasks deserve early attention. Task 22, evaluating emerging technologies, is where AI, cloud, OT, and quantum risk enter the exam. Task 24, facilitating tabletop exercises, frames the practitioner as a facilitator: someone who runs the exercise so that owners practice their own decisions, rather than someone who makes those decisions for them.

💡 Exam TipNotice the verbs. Tasks say identify, analyze, recommend, facilitate, monitor, and report. Very few say approve or accept. That pattern is the exam’s hint about who owns what.
5

The Risk Practitioner Mindset and Best-Answer Tactics

CRISC items reward a consistent professional lens. Four principles resolve most hard questions.

  • The owner decides. The practitioner advises. Responses, acceptance, and exceptions belong to the risk owner; appetite and risk beyond it belong to senior management and the board.
  • Business first. Risk exists because objectives exist. Start from the business objective, process, or asset at stake, not from the technology.
  • Assess before you respond. Identify and analyze before recommending a control. Buying a tool before you understand the risk is the classic wrong answer.
  • Process and ownership before tools. A policy, an owner, and a repeatable process come before automation.
KeywordWhat it asksHow to choose
FIRSTThe correct next step in a sequence.Prefer understanding (gather facts, identify, assess) over action. Several options may be right eventually; pick the one that must come before the others.
BESTThe most effective option overall.Prefer the option that addresses the root cause, fits the business, and keeps ownership where it belongs.
MOST important / MOST likelyThe option with the greatest weight or probability.Tie it to business objectives and impact, not to technical elegance.
PRIMARYThe main purpose or reason.Choose the reason at the highest level, often the business or governance reason, not a side benefit.
GREATEST concernThe worst weakness in the scenario.Look for missing ownership, missing appetite, or unassessed risk before a missing technical control.

An elimination routine

  • Read the last sentence first to find the keyword and the role being asked about.
  • Cross out options where the practitioner makes the owner’s decision.
  • Cross out options that jump to a solution before the risk is understood.
  • Between two survivors, choose the one closer to business objectives and accountability.

CRISC Among CISA, CISM, and CISSP

Faculty often hold or teach toward more than one of these credentials. They overlap in content but differ in lens, and the lens is what changes the best answer.

CredentialBodyCore questionTypical best-answer lens
CRISCISACAIs IT risk identified, assessed, owned, responded to, and reported within appetite?Risk practitioner: advise, facilitate, report; the owner decides.
CISAISACAAre controls designed and operating effectively, and is the evidence sufficient?Independent auditor: evidence, independence, reporting to the audit committee.
CISMISACAIs the information security program aligned with business goals and well governed?Security manager: program, strategy, incident management, business alignment.
CISSPISC2Is security designed, engineered, and operated soundly across eight domains?Security leader or manager with broad technical depth; protect people first.

The same scenario can have different best answers. Suppose a control fails a test. The CISA lens reports the finding with evidence. The CISM lens asks how the security program should fix it. The CRISC lens asks what the failure does to residual risk, whether that risk is still within appetite, and whether the risk owner knows.

💡 Exam TipIf you already hold CISA, watch for answers that make the practitioner the independent tester. If you hold CISM, watch for answers that make the practitioner the program owner. On CRISC, the practitioner is the advisor in the middle.

Interactive: Decision Owner Sorter

Ten decisions come from a normal year at Larchmont Community Health, a fictional regional clinic network. For each one, choose who owns it: the Board or Senior Management, the Risk Owner, the Control Owner, the Risk Practitioner, or Internal Audit. Explore mode starts with a new analyst’s first draft and grades each row as you change it. Click a row’s mark to see why. The chart compares how many decisions each role should own with how many you gave it, which makes over-reliance on the practitioner easy to spot. Challenge mode shuffles the rows, hides the grades, and asks for 9 or more correct.

🔎 In practice

The new analyst’s draft gets six of ten right, and its errors follow a common pattern. It lets a risk owner accept risk that is above appetite, hands the practitioner a control to design and a response to choose, and sends funding to the board even though the spend sits inside the department’s own authority. Each error is a governance failure in real life: decisions made by someone who cannot be held accountable for them, or pushed upward when the owner could decide.

💡 Exam TipThis sorter is the exam’s most common hidden test. When a question asks who should accept a risk, approve an exception, or choose a response, find the owner with authority. When it asks who should test a control independently, find internal audit. When it asks what the practitioner should do, look for analyze, recommend, facilitate, or report.

Faculty Teaching Segment

Before teaching CRISC content, decide where it lives in your program. Most programs already teach pieces of it; few teach it as one coherent risk practice. The map below shows common placements.

Program placementCRISC content that fitsWorkshop lessons
Intro to security or ITRisk vocabulary, roles and ownership, CIA, the risk cycleL0, L9
GRC or security management courseGovernance, appetite, frameworks, legal drivers, policyL1, L2
Risk assessment course or moduleThreats, scenarios, analysis methods, the risk registerL3, L4
Controls, audit, or compliance courseResponses, third parties, control design and testing, KRIsL5, L6, L7
Capstone or practicumEmerging technology risk, integrated case, tabletop facilitationL8, L9
  • Signature assignment: give students a one-page profile of a fictional college (Penmore Technical College) and a list of fifteen risk decisions, from approving the appetite statement to running backups and testing access reviews. Students build a RACI matrix that names who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed for each, then write a one-paragraph memo explaining the three placements they found hardest.
  • Rubric criteria: exactly one Accountable party per decision; acceptance and appetite placed with authority (risk owner or board), not with IT or the practitioner; independence preserved (internal audit not responsible for operating controls it tests); and a memo that justifies hard calls with governance reasoning rather than job titles.
  • Lab idea: students load a synthetic risk register of twenty entries into a spreadsheet, add owner and control-owner columns, and use filters to flag entries with no owner, the same person as risk owner and tester, or acceptance recorded by the practitioner. Use only synthetic data you supply.
  • Course fit: an introductory GRC, security management, or IT risk course. It supports the CAE-CD Cybersecurity Planning and Management (CPM), Policy, Legal, Ethics, and Compliance (PLE), and Cybersecurity Principles (CSP) Knowledge Units.
🎓 Teaching noteRun the sorter in pairs. Have each pair complete Challenge mode once, then hand out a printed copy of the ten decisions and ask them to write the RACI letters for every role, not just the owner. The discussion about who is Consulted and Informed is where students learn that the practitioner is involved in almost everything and accountable for very little.

Knowledge Check (self-check)

Test your understanding

Select the best answer for each question, then submit for your score and the rationale for every item. Like the exam, several options may be true; choose the best one.

Q1.A clinic manager wants to keep an unsupported scheduling server in service for another year. The risk analysis shows residual risk above the organization’s approved appetite. Who should decide whether to accept this risk?

Question 1 options
Correct: B. Risk above appetite exceeds the clinic manager’s delegated authority, so senior management (or the board, per policy) must accept it or direct treatment. The practitioner analyzes and recommends, the IT manager is a custodian, and internal audit provides assurance, not acceptance.

Q2.A faculty member has two years of risk and control work experience and wants to earn CRISC. Which plan BEST fits ISACA’s current requirements?

Question 2 options
Correct: D. Candidates may pass the exam first and apply within five years of passing. ISACA lists no experience waivers or substitutions for CRISC, so A and C are not available. B works but needlessly delays the exam.

Q3.While compiling quarterly KRIs, a risk practitioner notices that one department’s incident counts are far lower than its help desk tickets suggest. What should the practitioner do FIRST?

Question 3 options
Correct: A. Data validation is a practitioner task, and due care means confirming the discrepancy before acting on it. Silently replacing figures (B) bypasses the owner, escalating with an accusation (C) is premature, and publishing known-questionable data (D) violates the duty to report fully and accurately.

Q4.A candidate has limited study time and wants to focus on the domain that carries the MOST exam weight. Which domain should come first?

Question 4 options
Correct: C. Risk Response and Reporting is 32% of the exam. Governance is 26%, Risk Assessment 22%, and Technology and Security 20%. Governance is close enough that it should come second.

Q5.A business unit leader must choose between mitigating and transferring a data-loss risk. What is the risk practitioner’s PRIMARY role in this decision?

Question 5 options
Correct: A. The practitioner informs the owner’s decision with analysis and a recommendation. Selecting (B) or approving (C) takes the owner’s authority, and lowest residual risk is not always the best business choice. Independent testing (D) belongs to internal audit.

Q6.A colleague is updating an older CRISC study plan. Which statement BEST describes the change made in the outline in effect since November 2025?

Question 6 options
Correct: D. IT Risk Assessment became Risk Assessment and rose to 22%; Information Technology and Security became Technology and Security and fell to 20%. The outline still has four domains (A, B are wrong), and the weight moved the other way (C).

Q7.A new state privacy regulation will apply to student records next year. What should the college’s risk practitioner do FIRST?

Question 7 options
Correct: C. Assess before you respond. Understanding applicability and impact comes before choosing tools (A) or changing policy (B). Promising compliance (D) before any assessment is unsupported and misleads the board.

Q8.A CRISC holder earned 15 CPE hours this year and 125 hours over the current three-year cycle. Which statement is MOST accurate?

Question 8 options
Correct: B. Both minimums apply: at least 20 hours each year and 120 per cycle. A and D ignore the annual floor. C overstates the consequence; ISACA’s CPE policy describes how shortfalls are handled, so check it rather than assuming a retake.
