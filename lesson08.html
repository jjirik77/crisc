Day 5, Morning • Domain 4A

Technology and Security

Every IT risk eventually lands on a technology: an architecture that ages, a change that breaks production, a project that skips a security gate, a backup that will not restore, or a new tool the business adopted before anyone asked what could go wrong. Domain 4A asks the risk practitioner to understand enough about how technology is built and run to see where risk enters, and to bring emerging technology such as AI under the same disciplined process as everything else.

Session: Day 5, 9:00 to 12:00 Duration: ~3 hrs Exam Weight: 20% Knowledge Check: 8 Questions

Overview

Where risk enters the technology stack

The first three domains give the risk practitioner a process: govern, assess, respond, and monitor. Domain 4 tests whether that process reaches the places where technology risk is actually created. Architecture decisions set the risk profile for years. Operations practices decide whether a bad change is caught or shipped. The system development life cycle decides whether security is designed in or bolted on. Resilience planning decides whether an outage costs an hour or a week.

This lesson walks the technology stack from enterprise architecture to disaster recovery, then turns to emerging technology: artificial intelligence and generative AI, cloud services, operational technology and the Internet of Things, and the move to post-quantum cryptography. The running example is Halloway Foods, a regional food manufacturer upgrading its production line and piloting a generative AI customer assistant.

⚖️ Think like a risk practitionerNew technology does not get a new risk process. It gets the same one: tie it to a business objective, identify and assess the risk, recommend a response, and assign an owner. When a question offers “ban the technology” or “buy a tool for it” next to “assess the risk first,” the assessment is almost always the better answer.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Explain how enterprise architecture, roadmaps, and technical debt shape an organization’s IT risk profile. Bloom: Understand
  • Identify risk points in operations management (change, asset, incident, and problem) and in DevOps pipelines. Bloom: Apply
  • Analyze where risk enters each SDLC phase and each stage of the data lifecycle, and which control belongs there. Bloom: Analyze
  • Evaluate recovery strategies against RTO and RPO set by the business impact analysis. Bloom: Evaluate
  • Assess emerging technology initiatives (AI, cloud, OT/IoT, post-quantum) using recognized frameworks and recommend a governance tier. Bloom: Evaluate

Technology Principles and Enterprise Architecture

Enterprise architecture describes how the business, its data, its applications, and its infrastructure fit together today (the baseline) and how they should fit together later (the target). The gap between the two becomes a roadmap of projects. For the risk practitioner, the architecture is a map of concentration and dependency: where a single platform carries many processes, where data flows cross trust boundaries, and where old technology holds up new services.

  • TOGAF: The Open Group Architecture Framework. Its Architecture Development Method (ADM) cycles through architecture vision, business, information systems, and technology architectures, then opportunities, migration planning, implementation governance, and change management. Risk and security requirements belong in every phase, not just the technology one.
  • COBIT 2019 link: APO03 Managed Enterprise Architecture sets the governance objective. A risk practitioner checks that architecture decisions are traced to business goals and that exceptions to architecture standards are recorded as risks.
  • Technology roadmaps: a dated plan for adopting, upgrading, and retiring platforms. A roadmap that only adds technology and never retires any is a growing attack surface.
  • Technical debt: deferred work that makes systems harder and riskier to change. It compounds like financial debt, and like financial debt it should be visible on a register with an owner and a repayment plan.
  • End of life and end of support: when a vendor stops selling (EOL) and then stops patching (EOS) a product. Each EOS system needs a decision: replace, isolate, apply compensating controls, or formally accept the risk.
Architecture principleWhat it meansRisk it reduces
StandardizationFewer platforms, approved patterns, reference designsUnpatched one-off systems, skill gaps
Modularity and loose couplingComponents connect through defined interfacesCascading failure, risky big-bang changes
Secure by designSecurity requirements set at the vision phaseExpensive retrofits, control gaps
Resilience by designRedundancy and failover where the BIA requires itSingle points of failure
Data as an assetEach data domain has an owner and a classificationUngoverned copies, unclear accountability
🔎 In practice

Halloway Foods runs its packaging line on a supervisory control system installed in 2012. The vendor ended support in 2024. The architecture review shows that the new IoT sensor project would connect directly to that system. The risk practitioner records the EOS platform as a risk, links it to the IoT project, and asks the plant manager, as risk owner, to choose between replacement on the roadmap and segmentation with documented acceptance until then.

💡 Exam TipWhen a question describes a legacy system that cannot be patched, the BEST answer usually involves documenting the risk and getting a decision from the risk owner (replace, isolate, compensate, or accept). Quietly leaving it in place, or unilaterally shutting it down, both skip the owner.

Operations Management

Most outages and many breaches start in operations: an untested change, an unknown asset, an incident closed without a root cause. ITIL 4 and COBIT 2019 describe these practices in different words, but the risk logic is the same. Each practice is a control, and each produces evidence the risk practitioner can monitor.

PracticePurposeRisk if weakUseful indicator
Change enablementAssess, authorize, schedule, and review changesOutages, unauthorized changes, backdoorsUnauthorized or emergency changes as a share of all changes
IT asset managementKnow what exists, who owns it, and its lifecycle stateShadow IT, unpatched unknown devicesAssets discovered on the network that are not in the inventory
Configuration managementRecord approved baselines and relationships in a CMDBConfiguration drift, unclear blast radiusSystems out of compliance with their baseline
Incident managementRestore normal service quicklyLong outages, missed breach noticesMean time to restore; incidents breaching SLA
Problem managementFind and remove root causesThe same incident recurringRepeat incidents; open known errors past target date

Change risk in practice

  • Standard changes: low-risk, pre-approved, repeatable (for example, adding a user to an approved group). They still leave a record.
  • Normal changes: assessed and authorized by a change authority, with a test plan and a back-out plan.
  • Emergency changes: made fast to restore service or close an active threat, then reviewed after the fact. A high rate of emergency changes is a key risk indicator.
  • Segregation of duties: the person who writes a change should not be the only person who approves and deploys it. In automated pipelines, the pipeline enforces this through required reviews.

DevOps

DevOps joins development and operations so that small changes ship often through an automated pipeline. That can lower risk, because small changes are easier to test and roll back. It can also raise it, because a compromised pipeline deploys bad code at the same speed. The control shifts from a weekly change board to the pipeline itself: protected branches, required peer review, automated tests, signed builds, and logs that prove each step happened.

The SDLC and Project and Portfolio Management

The cheapest place to fix a security flaw is in the requirements. The most expensive place is in production. Whatever delivery model a project uses, the risk practitioner wants the same things: security and privacy requirements stated early, risk reviewed at each decision point, and nothing promoted to production without evidence that it was tested.

SDLC phaseTypical riskControl or gate
Initiation and feasibilityProject does not support a business objective; risk not considered in the business caseBusiness case with a risk section; portfolio approval
RequirementsSecurity, privacy, and compliance needs missingSecurity and privacy requirements; data classification; abuse cases
DesignArchitectural flaws, excessive trustThreat modeling; architecture review; secure design patterns
DevelopmentCoding flaws, vulnerable libraries, leaked secretsSecure coding standard; SAST; software composition analysis; secrets scanning
TestingDefects reach production; tests use real personal dataDAST; penetration testing; user acceptance; masked or synthetic test data
ImplementationUnauthorized or failed deploymentChange approval; segregation of duties; back-out plan
Operations and maintenanceDrift, new vulnerabilities, unpatched componentsVulnerability management; monitoring; periodic review
DisposalData left on retired systemsData migration, archiving, and sanitization with a record

Waterfall and Agile

WaterfallPhases run in sequence with formal sign-off between them. Risk reviews attach naturally to phase gates. The risk is late discovery: a flaw found in testing may force a redesign.
AgileWork ships in short iterations from a prioritized backlog. Risk work goes into the backlog as user stories and acceptance criteria, and into the definition of done. The risk is that security stories keep getting pushed to the next sprint.
  • DevSecOps gates: automated checks in the pipeline (static analysis, dependency scanning, container and infrastructure-as-code scanning, secrets detection) with thresholds that block a build. NIST SP 800-218, the Secure Software Development Framework (SSDF), is a useful reference for these practices.
  • Project management: each project keeps a risk log (delivery risk) that is separate from, but linked to, the enterprise risk register (business risk). A project that will change the risk profile must update the register before go-live.
  • Portfolio management: COBIT 2019 APO05 Managed Portfolio and BAI01 Managed Programs. The portfolio view lets leadership compare projects by value and risk and stop the ones that no longer support objectives.
🔎 In practice

The Halloway Foods assistant team works in two-week sprints. For three sprints in a row, the story “filter customer account numbers from prompts” was moved to the next sprint. The risk practitioner did not rewrite the backlog. Instead, the practitioner showed the product owner the open risk on the register and proposed adding “no personal data in prompt logs” to the definition of done. The product owner agreed, and the story was finished in the next sprint.

💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks when security requirements should be defined, the answer is the earliest phase offered, usually requirements or initiation. When it asks who decides whether a system with open risks goes live, the answer is the business owner, informed by the risk practitioner’s assessment.

Data Lifecycle Management

Data moves through six stages: create, store, use, share, archive, and destroy. Risk changes at each stage, and so do the controls. A data owner sets the requirements for each stage, and custodians operate the controls. The risk practitioner checks that requirements exist for every stage, not just storage.

StageKey riskTypical controls
Create or collectCollecting more than needed; wrong classificationData minimization; classification at creation; notice and lawful basis
StoreUnauthorized access; loss of integrityEncryption at rest; access control; backups; integrity checks
UseMisuse by authorized users; inferenceLeast privilege; logging and monitoring; purpose limitation
ShareLeakage to third parties; loss of controlData sharing agreements; DLP; encryption in transit; contract clauses
ArchiveUnreadable formats; forgotten copies; legal holds missedRetention schedule; format migration; archive access review
DestroyRemanence; destroying data still under holdSanitization per NIST SP 800-88; certificate of destruction; hold check
  • Data quality: risk decisions are only as good as the data behind them. Accuracy, completeness, and timeliness are control objectives in their own right, especially for data that trains AI models.
  • Data lineage: knowing where data came from and every system it passed through. Lineage is what lets an organization answer a regulator, honor a deletion request, or trace a poisoned training set.
  • Copies and derivatives: test environments, analytics extracts, and AI training sets are copies. They inherit the classification of the source unless the data has been properly de-identified.
🔖 NoteFor AI systems, the data lifecycle gains two new stages in practice: data used to train or tune a model, and data that the model generates. Both need an owner, a classification, and a retention rule.

Technology Resilience and Disaster Recovery

Resilience starts with the business impact analysis from Domain 2. The BIA sets the maximum tolerable downtime (MTD) for each process. Technology recovery targets must sit inside it: the recovery time objective (RTO) says how fast a system must be back, and the recovery point objective (RPO) says how much data loss is tolerable. The disaster recovery plan (DRP) restores technology. The business continuity plan (BCP) keeps the business running, including manual workarounds, while it does.

Recovery optionTypical recovery speedRelative costGood fit
Hot site or active-activeMinutes to hoursHighestOrder processing, payment systems, OT control with tight RTO
Warm siteHours to a day or twoModerateImportant internal systems
Cold siteDays to weeksLowNon-critical systems with long MTD
Cloud recovery (DR as a service)Hours, if pre-built and testedPay as usedVirtualized workloads; must match data residency rules
Reciprocal agreementUncertainLowRarely acceptable; hard to enforce and test

Backup strategy

  • Full, incremental, differential: a full backup copies everything. Incremental copies changes since the last backup of any kind (fast to take, slower to restore). Differential copies changes since the last full (slower to take, faster to restore).
  • 3-2-1 and beyond: at least three copies, on two types of media, with one off site. Against ransomware, add an immutable or offline copy that an attacker with administrator rights cannot delete.
  • Restore testing: a backup is only a hope until it has been restored. Test restores on a schedule and measure them against RTO and RPO.

Testing the plan

Plans are tested in increasing depth: checklist review, tabletop walkthrough, simulation, parallel test (recovery systems run alongside production), and full interruption test (production is shut down). Each step raises realism and risk. Full interruption tests are rare because the test itself can cause the outage it is meant to prevent.

💡 Exam TipIf the RTO is shorter than the time a recovery option actually takes, the plan does not meet the business requirement, no matter how good it looks on paper. When a question asks what to verify FIRST about a DRP, look for alignment with the BIA. When it asks for the BEST evidence that backups work, the answer is a successful restore test.
🔎 In practice

Halloway’s BIA sets an MTD of 8 hours for order processing, because grocery customers place orders for next-day delivery. The current DR design restores the order database from nightly tape at a cold site, a process that last took three days in a test. The gap goes on the risk register, and the CFO, as owner of order processing, approves funding for cloud-based recovery with replication every 15 minutes.

6

Emerging Technologies

Emerging technology is where the risk practitioner’s process is tested hardest, because the business wants speed and the evidence base is thin. The answer is not to block innovation. It is to apply the same discipline: inventory the use, tie it to an objective, assess the risk with a recognized framework, set a governance tier that matches the risk, and monitor as the technology matures.

Artificial intelligence and generative AI

FrameworkWhat it isHow a risk practitioner uses it
NIST AI RMF 1.0 (January 2023)Voluntary framework with four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and ManageStructure for an AI risk program: Govern sets culture and accountability; Map establishes context and risks; Measure assesses them; Manage prioritizes and responds
NIST AI 600-1 (July 2024)Generative AI Profile of the AI RMFCatalog of risks that are new or worse with GenAI, such as confabulation, data privacy, information integrity, and information security, with suggested actions
ISO/IEC 42001:2023Certifiable AI management system standardManagement system structure (policy, roles, risk assessment, impact assessment, controls, improvement) that fits alongside an ISO/IEC 27001 ISMS
EU AI ActRisk-based regulation with four tiers: unacceptable (prohibited), high-risk, limited risk (transparency duties), and minimal riskClassify each AI use by tier; obligations phase in from 2025 to 2027, so check the current dates for each obligation
  • Typical AI risk scenarios: sensitive data entered in prompts and retained by a provider; confident but wrong output (confabulation) acted on without review; prompt injection that makes an assistant ignore its instructions; biased outcomes in decisions about people; model drift as real-world data changes; and dependence on a single model provider.
  • Typical AI controls: an AI use inventory and acceptable use policy; data classification rules for prompts and training data; human-in-the-loop review for consequential outputs; output filtering and grounding in approved sources; model monitoring with KRIs for error and complaint rates; vendor due diligence on data use and retention; and a documented way to switch the system off.

Accountability never transfers. A SaaS provider runs the platform, but the customer still decides who has access, how data is classified, and whether the provider’s assurance evidence (a SOC 2 Type II report or ISO/IEC 27001 certificate, for example) actually covers the services in use. Exit planning matters too: can the data be retrieved in a usable format, and how fast?

Operational technology and IoT

Operational technology (OT) controls physical processes: mixers, ovens, conveyors, and refrigeration. Its priorities differ from IT. Availability and safety come first, systems run for decades, and patching may require a plant shutdown. Connecting IoT sensors to OT networks brings analytics benefits and a new path for attackers. Useful references are NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3, Guide to Operational Technology Security, and the ISA/IEC 62443 series. Typical controls include network segmentation between IT and OT zones, strict control of vendor remote access, asset inventory of every connected device, and monitoring designed for industrial protocols.

Post-quantum cryptography

A large enough quantum computer could break the public-key algorithms (RSA, elliptic-curve) that protect most key exchange and digital signatures today. Attackers can record encrypted traffic now and decrypt it later (“harvest now, decrypt later”), so data that must stay confidential for many years is already exposed to this risk. In August 2024, NIST published the first post-quantum standards: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) for key encapsulation, FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) for digital signatures, and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA), a hash-based signature scheme. The risk practitioner’s first job is not to pick algorithms. It is to make sure there is a cryptographic inventory, that data is ranked by how long it must stay secret, and that systems are being built for crypto-agility.

🔎 In practice

Halloway Foods has four technology proposals in front of its steering committee: a generative AI customer assistant, IoT sensors on the OT line, a migration to a SaaS ERP, and a quantum-vulnerable cryptography inventory. The committee asks the risk function for a consistent way to decide which ones need board attention. The interactive in the next section is that method.

💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks what an organization should do FIRST about employees using public generative AI tools, look for an inventory of current use and an acceptable use policy grounded in a risk assessment. A blanket ban drives use underground; unrestricted use ignores data risk.

Interactive: Emerging Technology Risk Profiler

Choose one of Halloway Foods’ four technology initiatives. Each is rated on six risk factors from 1 (low) to 5 (high), with defaults set from the case. The profiler converts the ratings to an overall score from 0 to 100, finds the top risk driver, and recommends a governance tier. The tier rule is deliberately simple: one factor at the maximum is enough for Enhanced review, and two factors at the maximum, or an overall score of 70 or more, send the initiative to the board. Change the ratings to see what moves the tier, and turn on Compare to overlay a second initiative’s profile.

    Faculty Teaching Segment

    Students arrive with strong opinions about AI and weak habits for assessing it. A structured use-case review on a real campus process turns opinion into evidence and gives them a reusable method.

    • Signature assignment: an AI use-case risk review for a college department. Teams receive a one-page proposal from a fictional department (for example, an admissions office that wants a GenAI assistant to draft replies to applicants). They classify the use under the EU AI Act tiers, map it to the four NIST AI RMF functions, rate the six profiler factors with a written reason for each, name the risk owner, and recommend a governance tier with three controls and two KRIs.
    • Rubric criteria: (1) risk factors rated with evidence from the proposal, not assumptions; (2) framework use accurate, including correct AI RMF functions and a defensible EU AI Act tier; (3) controls matched to the top drivers and feasible for the department; (4) ownership and escalation clear, with the business owner deciding and the risk function advising.
    • Lab idea: give students a synthetic prompt log of 200 entries from a fictional help-desk assistant, some containing fake student ID numbers and account details. Students write a simple filter rule, measure how many sensitive entries it catches and misses, and turn the miss rate into a KRI with thresholds. Use only synthetic data.
    • Course fit: an IT risk management or emerging technology course, or the technology module of a GRC course. It supports the CAE-CD Knowledge Units Cybersecurity Planning and Management (CPM); Policy, Legal, Ethics, and Compliance (PLE); Privacy (PRI); and Supply Chain Security (SCS).
    🎓 Teaching noteRun the profiler in pairs. Give each pair a printed scoring sheet with the six factors and ask them to rate one Halloway initiative before they open the tool, then compare. Disagreements of two points or more on a factor are the discussion: make each side state the evidence behind its number. Collect the sheets as the artifact.

    Knowledge Check (self-check)

    Test your understanding

    Select the best answer for each question, then submit for your score and the rationale for every item. Like the exam, several options may be true; choose the best one.

    Q1.A manufacturer’s marketing team has started using a public generative AI tool to draft customer emails that sometimes include order details. What should the risk practitioner do FIRST?

    Question 1 options
    Correct: C. The practitioner identifies and assesses before responding. Blocking and buying are responses chosen before the risk is understood, and either may be right later. Disciplinary action assumes a policy existed and was breached, which the stem does not say.

    Q2.The same application outage has occurred four times this quarter. Each time, the service desk restored service within its SLA. Which action BEST addresses the risk?

    Question 2 options
    Correct: A. Recurring incidents point to an unresolved root cause, which is the purpose of problem management. A shorter SLA and faster detection reduce impact but not frequency. DR planning addresses major disruption, not a recurring defect.

    Q3.A business impact analysis sets a maximum tolerable downtime of 8 hours for order processing. The disaster recovery plan restores the order system from offsite backups in about 36 hours. What is the MOST appropriate next step?

    Question 3 options
    Correct: D. The recovery capability does not meet the business requirement. The owner needs the gap and costed options to decide. A full interruption test is risky and does not close the gap. More frequent backups improve RPO, not recovery time. Changing the MTD to fit the plan reverses the logic of the BIA.

    Q4.An organization is moving its HR system to a SaaS provider. Which responsibility remains with the organization under the shared responsibility model?

    Question 4 options
    Correct: B. In SaaS, the provider runs the infrastructure, platform, and application. The customer always keeps responsibility for its data and for identity and access decisions. The other three are provider duties in a SaaS model.

    Q5.During a sprint review, a product owner proposes deferring a security acceptance criterion for the fourth consecutive sprint. What is the risk practitioner’s BEST course of action?

    Question 5 options
    Correct: A. The practitioner advises and the owner decides, with the decision recorded. Halting the project and editing the backlog both take decisions the practitioner does not own. Going straight to the board skips the owner and normal escalation; it would follow only if the risk exceeded appetite and the owner would not act.

    Q6.An organization holds supplier contracts that must remain confidential for 15 years. Which action BEST addresses the risk that quantum computing poses to this data?

    Question 6 options
    Correct: C. Long-lived data is exposed now to harvest-now, decrypt-later attacks. An inventory plus a migration plan toward NIST’s post-quantum standards (such as FIPS 203 ML-KEM) is the structured response. Longer RSA keys do not resist a quantum attack in the long run, waiting ignores data already being collected, and a vendor claim is not an assessment.

    Q7.A food plant wants to connect wireless sensors on its production line to the vendor’s cloud analytics service. The line’s control system is past end of support. Which control is MOST important to require?

    Question 7 options
    Correct: B. Segmentation limits the new path from the internet to an unpatchable system, which is the core risk. Sensors rarely support antivirus. An end-of-support system cannot be patched, which is the problem. Awareness helps but does not address the network exposure.

    Q8.Which framework would a risk practitioner use to organize an AI risk program around the functions Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage?

    Question 8 options
    Correct: D. The NIST AI RMF 1.0 is built on those four functions. ISO/IEC 42001 is a certifiable AI management system standard with a different structure. CSF 2.0 uses six functions (Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover). The EU AI Act is a regulation organized by risk tier.
