Day 1, Late Morning and Afternoon • Domain 1A

Organizational Governance

Every IT risk is a risk to something the business is trying to achieve. Governance decides what that is, who is accountable for it, and how the organization will know it is on track. This lesson builds the context that the rest of CRISC depends on: strategy and goals, structure and roles, culture, policy, resilient processes, and the assets that support them.

Session: Day 1, 10:30 to 5:00 Duration: ~5 hrs Exam Weight: 26% (Domain 1) Knowledge Check: 8 Questions

Overview

Context before controls

A risk practitioner cannot rate a risk without knowing what it threatens. Organizational governance supplies that context. The board and senior management set direction and priorities. Structures and roles assign accountability. Culture and policy shape behavior. Business processes and the assets beneath them are where risk actually materializes. Domain 1A asks whether you can read that context and use it.

This lesson separates governance from management using COBIT 2019, follows the goals cascade from stakeholder needs to IT objectives, and covers organizational structure and RACI, segregation of duties, risk culture and ethics, the policy hierarchy and its exceptions, business processes and resilience, and organizational asset management. The interactive asks you to align a real-looking IT portfolio with enterprise goals and spot both unaligned spend and uncovered goals.

⚖️ Think like a risk practitionerStart from business objectives, not from technology. When an IT initiative cannot be traced to an enterprise goal, that is a governance finding to report, not a project for the practitioner to cancel. When an enterprise goal has nothing supporting it, that is a strategic risk to raise with the people who set the goals.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Distinguish governance from management using the COBIT 2019 governance and management domains. Bloom: Understand
  • Trace IT initiatives to enterprise goals through the goals cascade and identify unaligned spend and coverage gaps. Bloom: Analyze
  • Apply RACI and segregation of duties to an organizational structure and recommend compensating controls where separation is not possible. Bloom: Apply
  • Evaluate risk culture, policy exceptions, and resilience arrangements for governance weaknesses. Bloom: Evaluate
  • Explain how asset inventory, ownership, classification, and valuation support risk assessment. Bloom: Understand
  • Design a board-brief assignment in which students translate an IT roadmap into enterprise-goal language. Bloom: Create

1

Governance vs. Management

Governance and management are easy to blur, and CRISC questions often test the line between them. Governance decides what the organization should achieve and whether it is achieving it. Management decides how and does the work. The board governs; executives manage.

COBIT 2019, ISACA’s framework for the governance and management of enterprise information and technology, draws this line with five domains and 40 governance and management objectives.

COBIT 2019 domainTypeFocusRisk example
EDM: Evaluate, Direct and MonitorGovernanceEvaluate options, set direction, monitor resultsThe board approves risk appetite and reviews the risk profile.
APO: Align, Plan and OrganizeManagementStrategy, architecture, budgets, people, risk, securityManagement maintains the risk register and the risk management process.
BAI: Build, Acquire and ImplementManagementProjects, requirements, solutions, changeA project team builds security requirements into a new system.
DSS: Deliver, Service and SupportManagementOperations, service requests, incidents, continuityOperations runs backups and handles incidents.
MEA: Monitor, Evaluate and AssessManagementPerformance, internal control, compliance, assuranceManagement monitors control performance and compliance.

Governance bodies

  • Board of directors (or trustees): sets strategy and risk appetite, oversees management, and is ultimately accountable. Committees do much of the detailed work.
  • Audit committee: oversees financial reporting, internal control, and internal and external audit. It protects audit independence.
  • Risk committee: where present, oversees the enterprise risk profile and management’s risk framework.
  • IT strategy committee: a board-level group that advises the board on the strategic role of IT and major investments.
  • IT steering committee: a management-level group of business and IT leaders that prioritizes and approves projects and monitors delivery. It manages; it does not govern.
💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks who is ultimately accountable for IT risk or for governance of enterprise IT, the answer is the board and senior management, not the CIO, the CISO, or the risk function.
🔎 In practice

Larchmont Community Health, a regional clinic network moving to a cloud electronic health record (EHR), had its IT steering committee approving the risk appetite for patient data. The risk practitioner pointed out that appetite is a governance decision. The board’s quality and risk committee now approves appetite, and the steering committee applies it to project decisions.

2

Strategy, Goals, Objectives, and the Goals Cascade

Strategy states where the organization wants to go. Goals state what success looks like. Objectives are specific, measurable targets that move it toward the goals. Risk is defined against all three: the effect of uncertainty on objectives. That is why a risk practitioner begins with the business plan, not the network diagram.

The COBIT 2019 goals cascade

The goals cascade turns broad stakeholder needs into specific IT priorities. It runs in four steps.

LevelWhat it statesLarchmont example
Stakeholder drivers and needsPressures and expectations from patients, regulators, funders, staffRural patients need care closer to home; regulators expect strong privacy.
Enterprise goalsWhat the organization must achieve (COBIT offers 13 generic goals)Improve patient safety; grow through telehealth.
Alignment goalsWhat information and technology must deliver (13 generic goals)Reliable, secure clinical applications; IT compliance with laws.
Governance and management objectivesWhich of the 40 objectives need priorityManaged risk, managed security, managed continuity.

The cascade works in both directions. Top down, it tells IT what to prioritize. Bottom up, it lets anyone ask of a project, “Which enterprise goal does this serve?” An initiative with no answer is unaligned spend. A goal with no initiative is a strategic gap.

  • Business case: every significant investment should state the goal it serves, the benefits, the costs, and the risks, so the steering committee can compare initiatives on the same terms.
  • Portfolio view: governance looks at the whole set of initiatives, because the right question is not only “Is this project good?” but also “Is this the best use of limited money across our goals?”
  • Metrics: each goal needs measures. Without them, alignment claims cannot be checked.
💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks for the PRIMARY reason to align IT with business strategy, choose the option about IT investments delivering value toward enterprise goals. Lower cost, better security, or faster delivery may follow, but they are not the primary purpose.
🧭 Practitioner’s noteUnaligned spend is a finding, not a verdict. The practitioner reports it to the steering committee or sponsor and asks for a business case. Whether the project continues is a management decision.
3

Organizational Structure, Roles, and Responsibilities

Accountability only works when it is written down. Structures (functional, divisional, matrix, or a mix) shape who reports to whom, and every structure needs clear answers about who decides, who does, and who checks.

RACI in practice

ActivityBoardRisk owner (business)Control ownerRisk practitionerInternal audit
Set risk appetiteACIRI
Select a risk responseIACRI
Operate a control IA/RI 
Report the risk profileICCA/RI
Independently test controlsIICIA/R

Here “R” for the practitioner on appetite means it drafts and facilitates; the board remains Accountable. Each row has exactly one A. Two As mean nobody is truly accountable.

Key roles

  • Chief risk officer (CRO): leads enterprise risk management and the second-line risk function; reports to the CEO and often to a board committee.
  • CIO and CISO: manage technology and security. They often own controls, and sometimes own technology risks, but they do not set enterprise appetite.
  • Business process owners: usually the risk owners for risks to their processes.
  • Data and system owners: decide classification and access for the assets they are accountable for. Custodians operate the controls.

Segregation of duties

Segregation of duties separates four incompatible functions: authorizing a transaction, recording it, holding custody of the asset, and verifying the result. In IT, common conflicts include a developer who can deploy to production, an administrator who can change audit logs, and a clerk who can create vendors and approve payments.

PreferredSeparate the duties across people or teams, and enforce the separation in system roles.
When you cannotIn small units, use compensating controls: independent review of logs and change reports, dual approval, job rotation, or mandatory vacations.
💡 Exam TipIf a question says separation is not feasible because the team is small, the BEST answer is usually an independent review or monitoring control by someone outside the conflict, not accepting the risk without comment.
4

Organizational Culture and Ethics

Policies describe intended behavior. Culture determines actual behavior. A risk-aware culture is one where people raise bad news early, follow controls when no one is watching, and see risk management as part of doing the job well.

SignalHealthy risk cultureWeak risk culture
Tone at the topLeaders follow the same rules and talk about risk in decisions.Leaders grant themselves informal exceptions.
Bad newsIncidents and near misses are reported quickly, without blame.Problems surface only through audits or outages.
IncentivesGoals and bonuses reward sound risk-taking, not only speed.Targets reward delivery even when controls are skipped.
ExceptionsExceptions are rare, documented, and expire.Exceptions are routine and never reviewed.
TrainingRole-based and tied to real scenarios.Annual click-through with no follow-up.

Ethics programs

  • Code of conduct: states expected behavior and how conflicts of interest are disclosed.
  • Reporting channels: confidential and anti-retaliation channels for concerns, often called whistleblower or ethics hotlines.
  • Consequences: consistent, visible responses to violations at every level.
  • Emerging issues: ethics now includes how the organization uses AI and data. ISACA highlights AI data governance and ethics as areas a CRISC holder should be ready to address.
💡 Exam TipWhen repeated control failures share a pattern across many teams, the MOST likely root cause is cultural or governance-level (tone at the top, incentives, unclear accountability), not one person’s mistake. Choose the answer that addresses the cause.
🔎 In practice

At Larchmont, clinicians shared EHR logins to save time during busy shifts. Retraining alone did not stop it. The risk practitioner’s analysis showed the root cause was slow log-in on shared workstations. Leadership funded tap-badge sign-on and publicly committed to it, and sharing dropped. The culture changed because leaders removed the incentive to break the rule.

5

Policies and Standards

Policies translate governance decisions into rules. A clear hierarchy keeps them consistent and makes each document easy to change at the right level.

DocumentPurposeMandatory?Approved by
PolicyHigh-level statement of intent and directionYesBoard or senior management
StandardSpecific, measurable requirements that support a policyYesManagement (e.g., CIO, CISO)
ProcedureStep-by-step instructions to meet a standardYes, for those performing itProcess or control owner
GuidelineRecommended practice and adviceNoSubject-matter owner
BaselineMinimum secure configuration for a system typeYesManagement, via the standard

Exceptions and exemptions

No policy fits every case. A controlled exception process is a sign of mature governance, not weak governance.

  • Exception: a temporary, approved deviation. It needs a business reason, a risk assessment, compensating controls, an expiry date, and acceptance by the risk owner with the right authority.
  • Exemption: a longer-term, formally approved release from a requirement, often because it does not apply or cannot be met. It is still documented and periodically re-reviewed.
  • Tracking: the risk function keeps a register of exceptions and exemptions, reports their volume and age, and escalates expired ones.
  • Review cycle: policies are reviewed on a set schedule and after major changes in strategy, law, or technology.

6

Business Processes and Resilience

Risk happens in processes. Before a practitioner can judge the impact of an outage or breach, the practitioner needs to know which processes matter most, what they depend on, and how long the organization can live without them.

Process mapping

  • Map the flow: inputs, steps, outputs, and the people and systems at each step. Swimlane diagrams show handoffs, which is where many controls and many failures sit.
  • Find dependencies: applications, data, infrastructure, facilities, suppliers, and key people.
  • Mark control points: where approvals, reconciliations, and checks happen.

The BIA and recovery objectives

A business impact analysis (BIA) estimates how harm grows over time when a process stops. It sets priorities for continuity and recovery. Lesson 4 covers the BIA in depth; the key outputs are these.

TermMeaningSet by
Maximum tolerable downtime (MTD)The longest a process can be unavailable before harm becomes unacceptableBusiness owner, from the BIA
Recovery time objective (RTO)The target time to restore a process or system; must be shorter than MTDBusiness owner, with IT input
Recovery point objective (RPO)The maximum acceptable data loss, measured in timeBusiness owner; drives backup frequency

BCP, DRP, and crisis communications

PlanScopeExample
Business continuity plan (BCP)Keeps critical business processes running during and after a disruptionClinics switch to paper downtime forms and phone scheduling.
Disaster recovery plan (DRP)Restores technology (systems, data, infrastructure) to support the BCPFail the EHR over to a secondary cloud region within the RTO.
Crisis communications planWho says what to whom, and when: staff, patients, regulators, mediaPre-approved patient notices and a single spokesperson.
Incident response planDetects, contains, and recovers from security incidentsIsolate an infected clinic network segment.

Recognized references include ISO 22301 for business continuity management systems and NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1 for federal contingency planning. Plans that are never tested are assumptions. Tabletop exercises, which the CRISC outline names as a supporting task, are the cheapest way to find gaps.

7

Organizational Asset Management

Risk assessment needs a list of what can be harmed. Asset management provides it: an inventory of information and technology assets, each with an owner, a classification, and a value. Without it, risk ratings are guesses and controls protect whatever happens to be visible.

  • Inventory scope: hardware, software and licenses, data sets, cloud services and accounts, network connections, facilities, suppliers, and key people. Shadow IT and unmanaged cloud services are the usual blind spots.
  • Ownership: every asset has a named owner who decides classification, access, and acceptable risk. An unowned asset has no one to accept its risk.
  • Classification: sensitivity and criticality labels that drive handling and protection. A system takes the classification of the most sensitive data it holds.
  • Configuration management database (CMDB): records configuration items and their relationships, so the impact of a failure or change can be traced to the services and processes that depend on it.
  • Valuation: combine replacement cost, the revenue or service the asset supports, regulatory and contractual consequences of its loss, and reputational effect. Value drives impact ratings in Domain 2.
  • Lifecycle: acquisition, deployment, maintenance, change, and disposal with sanitization. End-of-support assets need replacement or documented risk acceptance.

Frameworks agree on this foundation. ISO/IEC 27001:2022 and ISO/IEC 27002:2022 include controls for an inventory of information and associated assets and for their ownership. NIST CSF 2.0 places asset management in its Identify function.

💡 Exam TipIf a question asks what a risk practitioner needs FIRST to assess risk to a new business unit, look for the asset inventory and business process information. You cannot rate impact on assets you have not identified and valued.
🔎 In practice

Larchmont’s EHR migration plan listed 140 servers. A discovery scan found 31 more, including a lab analyzer interface no one had assigned an owner. The practitioner added each to the CMDB with an owner and a classification before the migration risk assessment began, which changed two of the top five migration risks.

IT’s draft looks reasonable at first glance: 87% of the budget appears aligned. But it filed the barcode scanners under no goal because clinical engineering would buy them, so patient safety, the network’s first goal, shows no support at all. 💡 Exam TipTwo findings come out of a goals cascade review: spend without a goal and goals without spend. The practitioner reports both to the people who govern the portfolio. Faculty Teaching Segment

Students in technical programs often struggle to explain why a project matters to anyone outside IT. A board brief forces the translation from technology to enterprise goals, which is the core skill of this domain.

  • Signature assignment: give students a one-page IT roadmap for a fictional organization (eight initiatives with budgets) and a strategic plan with four enterprise goals. Students write a two-page board brief that maps each initiative to a goal, flags unaligned spend and uncovered goals, and recommends what the steering committee should ask the sponsors. They submit the mapping as a spreadsheet and the brief as a document.
  • Rubric criteria: each initiative mapped to a goal with evidence from its business case; unaligned spend and coverage gaps identified and quantified; recommendations that keep decisions with the steering committee and sponsors rather than the analyst; and plain business language a board member could act on, with no unexplained technical terms.
  • Lab idea: students build the mapping in a spreadsheet with a pivot table or chart that totals budget by goal, then change two assumptions (a cancelled project and a new regulation) and report how the alignment picture shifts. Use only the synthetic roadmap you supply.
  • Course fit: a GRC, IT management, or security management course, or the governance unit of a capstone. It supports the CAE-CD Cybersecurity Planning and Management (CPM), Policy, Legal, Ethics, and Compliance (PLE), and Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery (BCD) Knowledge Units.
🎓 Teaching noteGive pairs a printed handout of the seven initiatives and their business cases before they open the aligner. Each pair commits to a mapping on paper first, then enters it and compares with the model. Spend the debrief on the two disputed rows, the EHR alerts and the barcode scanners, and ask what evidence in a business case would settle the argument.

Scored Knowledge Check

Test your understanding

Select the best answer for each question, then submit for your score and the rationale for every item. Like the exam, several options may be true; choose the best one.

Q1.Which activity is the BEST example of governance rather than management of enterprise IT?

Question 1 options
Correct: C. Evaluating, directing, and monitoring at the board level is governance (COBIT EDM). Patching (DSS), project tracking (BAI), and control performance monitoring by the CISO (MEA) are management activities, even though D sounds like oversight.

Q2.During a portfolio review, a risk practitioner finds a $260,000 technology pilot with no sponsor and no link to any enterprise goal. What is the BEST action?

Question 2 options
Correct: A. Unaligned spend is a governance finding for the body that prioritizes investments. The practitioner has no authority to stop the pilot (B). Retrofitting a goal (C) hides the problem. Registering it as a risk (D) may follow, but does not get the decision to the people who must make it.

Q3.What is the PRIMARY purpose of the COBIT 2019 goals cascade?

Question 3 options
Correct: D. The cascade links stakeholder needs to specific, actionable priorities for information and technology. Ranking by ROI (A), audit testing (B), and risk ownership (C) are related activities, but none is the cascade’s purpose.

Q4.At a small clinic, one billing clerk can both add vendors to the payment system and approve vendor payments. Hiring another clerk is not possible. Which control BEST addresses the risk?

Question 4 options
Correct: B. When duties cannot be separated, an independent review by someone outside the conflict is the standard compensating control. Training (A) and stronger authentication (D) do not address the conflict. Acceptance (C) may be an option for the risk owner, but not before a feasible compensating control is considered.

Q5.Across several departments, staff routinely bypass the access-request process with informal approval from senior managers. What is the MOST likely root cause?

Question 5 options
Correct: D. The pattern spans departments and involves senior managers, which points to tone at the top. Usability (A) and training (B) may contribute, but they do not explain why managers approve the bypass. An outdated standard (C) would not cause managers to ignore it.

Q6.A department needs to keep using a legacy lab device that cannot meet the encryption standard for six more months. What is the BEST way to handle this?

Question 6 options
Correct: A. A time-limited, documented exception with compensating controls and owner acceptance is the governed path. Weakening the standard for one device (B) affects every system. Disconnecting (C) may halt a critical process without a risk decision. Informal tolerance (D) leaves the risk unowned.

Q7.A clinic network is starting continuity planning. Which should be completed FIRST?

Question 7 options
Correct: B. The BIA identifies critical processes and sets MTD, RTO, and RPO. The DRP (A), exercises (C), and recovery sites (D) all depend on those priorities and would otherwise be built on guesses.

Q8.A risk practitioner finds that the asset inventory lists systems and locations but has no owner field. What is the GREATEST concern?

Question 8 options
Correct: C. Ownership is what connects an asset to risk decisions. Without owners, classification, access approval, and risk acceptance have no accountable party. Licensing (A), relationships (B), and depreciation (D) are real but narrower issues.
