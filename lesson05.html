Day 3, Afternoon • Domain 3A

Risk Response

Analysis tells the risk owner how big a risk is. Response decides what to do about it. This lesson covers the four response options, how to justify a response by cost and residual risk, who owns which decision, how to manage risk that lives with vendors and their suppliers, and how to handle the issues, findings, exceptions, and exemptions that every program accumulates.

Session: Day 3, 1:00 to 4:00 Duration: ~3 hrs Exam Weight: 32% Knowledge Check: 8 Questions

Overview

Bring residual risk within appetite, at a cost the business can justify

Every risk response has the same goal: move residual risk to a level the enterprise is willing to accept, without spending more than the reduction is worth. Sometimes that means a new control. Sometimes it means insurance, a contract clause, or walking away from an activity. Sometimes it means a documented decision to live with the risk. None of these is the default answer; each is right in some situations and wrong in others.

Domain 3 carries the largest share of the exam, and response is where its judgment questions concentrate. This lesson builds the decision logic, then applies it to Corveth Logistics, a fictional freight company whose telematics vendor has a SOC 2 exception. The Risk Response Decision Console lets you compare all five response choices on one chart.

⚖️ Think like a risk practitionerThe risk practitioner recommends; the risk owner decides. A response is selected for a business reason, not because a control is available. Transfer moves money, not accountability. And acceptance is a decision made at the right level of authority, written down, and revisited, never a default for risks nobody wanted to discuss.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Distinguish accept, mitigate, transfer or share, and avoid, and the conditions under which each is appropriate. Bloom: Understand
  • Calculate residual ALE, annual response cost, and total cost of risk to compare responses. Bloom: Apply
  • Assign risk ownership and control ownership, and document a risk acceptance at the right level of authority. Bloom: Apply
  • Evaluate third-party risk across the vendor lifecycle, including SOC 2 reports, contract clauses, fourth parties, and SBOMs. Bloom: Evaluate
  • Differentiate issues, findings, exceptions, and exemptions, and route each through the correct process. Bloom: Analyze
  • Design a third-party due-diligence assignment built on a student-written SOC 2-style summary. Bloom: Create

Risk Response Options and Selection Criteria

The CRISC outline, the ISACA Risk IT Framework, and ISO 31000:2018 describe the same four basic choices, with slightly different names. ISO 31000 calls the step “risk treatment” and lists more variations, but they all reduce to the four below. Real responses often combine them: mitigate first, then transfer part of what remains, then accept the rest.

OptionWhat it doesWhen it fitsCorveth example
AcceptRetains the risk at its current level, with a documented decision.Risk is within appetite, or the cost of any response exceeds the benefit and an authorized person agrees.A low-value internal dashboard runs on an older library; the owner accepts it for one year.
MitigateReduces likelihood, impact, or both with controls.A cost-effective control can bring residual risk within appetite.Require phishing-resistant MFA and token rotation on the telematics vendor portal.
Transfer or shareMoves part of the financial impact to an insurer or a contract partner.Impact is severe but infrequent, and the loss is mostly financial and insurable.Cyber insurance that covers breach response and notification costs.
AvoidStops the activity that creates the risk.The risk exceeds appetite, no response reduces it enough at reasonable cost, and the activity is not essential.Stop sending driver personal data to the vendor, or exit the service.

Selection criteria

  • Appetite and tolerance: the response must bring residual risk within limits the board has set. This is the first test, not the last.
  • Cost and benefit: the reduction in expected loss should exceed the cost of the response over its life, including operating costs, not just purchase price.
  • Effect on objectives: a response that removes risk by crippling the business process fails the business, even when it succeeds on paper.
  • Legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements: some controls are mandatory regardless of cost-benefit, such as PCI DSS v4.0 requirements for card data.
  • Capability and timing: can the organization implement and operate the response, and how long will exposure remain until it does?
  • Side effects and new risks: every response can create secondary risk, such as a new vendor, a more complex system, or an insurance exclusion.
💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks for the PRIMARY consideration in selecting a response, look for alignment with risk appetite and business objectives, or cost-benefit. Answers that name a specific tool, “industry best practice,” or “the most secure option” are usually distractors.

Cost-Benefit Analysis and Residual Risk

A response is worth funding when the risk it removes is worth more than it costs. In annual terms:

  • Value of a response: ALE before the response, minus ALE after the response, minus the annual cost of the response.
  • Total cost of risk: residual ALE plus annual response cost. Comparing options on this single number shows which one leaves the business best off, provided residual risk is also within appetite.
  • Annual cost: includes licenses, staff time, operating overhead, and the amortized cost of implementation. A cheap tool that needs a full-time analyst is not cheap.

Cost-benefit is necessary but not sufficient. The lowest total cost of risk can still leave residual risk above appetite, for example when a cheap insurance policy leaves a large reputational loss uncovered. The two tests are applied together: first appetite, then cost.

Cyber insurance: what it covers and what it does not

Policy termMeaningRisk practitioner’s question
PremiumThe annual price of the policy.Is it a cost of transfer that belongs in the cost-benefit comparison?
Deductible or retentionThe part of each loss the insured pays first.Can we absorb this amount for every claim, perhaps more than once a year?
Limit and sublimitsThe maximum the insurer pays, overall and for specific loss types.Does the limit match the tail of our loss distribution, not just the average?
ExclusionsEvents or conditions the policy does not cover.Do any exclusions match our top scenarios, such as failures to maintain required controls?
Conditions and warrantiesControls the insured promises to maintain, such as MFA or backups.If a control fails, will a claim be denied?

Insurance pays for financial loss. It does not restore member trust, prevent customers from leaving, or reduce the enterprise’s accountability to regulators. Those impacts remain, which is why insurance alone rarely brings a severe reputational risk within appetite.

🔎 In practice

Corveth’s CFO proposed buying a larger cyber policy instead of funding vendor-access controls because the premium was lower than the control budget. The risk practitioner showed that the policy covered notification and forensics but not the lost shipping contracts that would follow a public breach. Residual risk stayed above appetite even with the policy in place. The final response combined two controls with a smaller policy.

💡 Exam TipIf a question asks what remains with the organization after it buys insurance, the answer is accountability (and usually reputational impact). Insurance transfers financial loss, never ownership of the risk.

Risk and Control Ownership

Two kinds of ownership run through every response. The risk owner is accountable for the risk: deciding the response, accepting residual risk, and answering for the outcome. The control owner is accountable for a specific control: making sure it is designed well, operates as intended, and produces evidence. One risk often depends on several controls with different owners, and one control often reduces several risks.

ActivityRisk ownerControl ownerRisk practitionerInternal audit
Select the response optionACR (recommends)I
Accept residual riskAICI
Design and operate the controlIA / RCI
Monitor KRIs and report statusICRI
Independently test the controlICIA / R

R = responsible, A = accountable, C = consulted, I = informed. This is an illustrative pattern; each enterprise sets its own RACI.

Documenting a risk acceptance

  • The risk: scenario, current and residual rating, and how it compares with appetite.
  • The decision maker: the risk owner, plus senior management or the board when residual risk exceeds appetite or the owner’s delegated authority.
  • The rationale: why other responses were rejected, with costs considered.
  • Conditions: compensating measures, KRIs to watch, and triggers that would reopen the decision.
  • A review or expiry date: acceptance is not permanent. Risks and businesses change.
📌 Authority mattersAn acceptance signed by someone without the authority to accept that level of risk is not an acceptance. It is an undocumented exposure with a signature on it. Delegation of authority should state who may accept which levels of residual risk.
💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks who should accept a risk, choose the risk owner (a business manager with the authority and accountability), not IT, the CISO, or the risk practitioner. If the risk exceeds appetite, look for escalation to senior management.
4

Vendor and Supply Chain Risk

Outsourcing a service does not outsource the risk. When Corveth sends shipment and driver data to a telematics vendor, the vendor operates the controls, but Corveth still answers to its customers and regulators if the data is exposed. Third-party risk management (TPRM) follows the vendor relationship from the first idea to the final exit.

Lifecycle stageKey activities
Planning and tieringDefine the business need; rate the vendor’s criticality and data sensitivity to set the depth of review (for example, Tier 1 critical to Tier 3 low).
Due diligenceSecurity questionnaires, SOC 2 or ISO/IEC 27001 certification evidence, financial health, ownership, location of data and support, fourth parties, incident history.
ContractingSecurity requirements, right to audit, breach notification timelines, subcontractor approval, data location, return and destruction of data, SLAs, indemnity, insurance requirements, termination assistance.
OnboardingLeast-privilege access, integration security review, named contacts on both sides, inclusion in the risk register.
Ongoing monitoringAnnual reassessment by tier, new SOC reports, security ratings, SLA and KRI tracking, news and breach monitoring, review of changes in fourth parties.
OffboardingRevoke access, retrieve or certify destruction of data, transition the service, close contract obligations, update the inventory.

Reading a SOC 2 report

  • Type I vs Type II: a Type I report covers the design of controls at a point in time. A Type II report also tests operating effectiveness over a period, so it is far stronger evidence.
  • Scope and period: confirm the system you use is in scope, and that the period is recent. A bridge (gap) letter from the vendor covers the time since the period ended.
  • Exceptions: the auditor lists controls that did not operate as described. Read each one and the vendor’s response, and judge the impact on your own risk.
  • Complementary user entity controls (CUECs): controls the vendor assumes you operate, such as reviewing your own users’ access. If you do not perform them, the vendor’s controls may not protect you.
  • Subservice organizations: the vendor’s own providers (fourth parties). A carve-out means their controls are excluded from the report and need separate assurance.

Software supply chain

A software bill of materials (SBOM) lists the components inside a product. When a widely used library has a critical vulnerability, an SBOM lets the enterprise answer “are we affected?” in hours rather than weeks. Common SBOM formats include SPDX and CycloneDX. Ask critical software vendors whether they provide SBOMs and how quickly they notify customers of vulnerable components.

🔎 In practice

The telematics vendor’s SOC 2 Type II report included one exception: quarterly access reviews for production systems were not performed in two of four quarters. The report also listed a CUEC requiring Corveth to review its own users on the vendor portal, which Corveth had never done. The risk practitioner recorded both gaps, recommended that the vendor relationship owner request a remediation plan and a bridge letter, and added a KRI for overdue access reviews.

💡 Exam TipWhen a vendor’s assurance report shows an exception, the FIRST step is usually to evaluate its impact on the enterprise’s own risk, including whether compensating or complementary controls exist. Terminating the contract or ignoring the exception are both premature.

Issues, Findings, Exceptions, and Exemptions

Every risk program generates a stream of known problems. Calling them by the right name matters, because each one follows a different path to closure.

TermWhat it isSourceHow it is handled
IssueA known problem or gap that needs action, whatever its source.Incidents, self-assessments, monitoring, staff reportsLogged with an owner and a due date, tracked to closure, linked to the affected risks.
FindingA documented result of an audit, assessment, or test that shows a control gap or noncompliance.Internal or external audit, regulators, assessorsManagement response, action plan, owner, due date, and validation by the party that raised it.
ExceptionAn approved, time-boxed deviation from a policy or standard.A business need that cannot meet the requirement yetRisk assessed, compensating controls defined, approved by the right authority, expiry date set, reviewed before renewal.
ExemptionA formally approved, longer-term exclusion from a requirement.A requirement that does not fit the situation for the foreseeable futureApproved at a higher level than an exception, documented with rationale, reviewed periodically rather than expiring on a short timer.
Exception: temporary“This legacy server cannot take the required patch for 90 days. We will isolate it on its own network segment and monitor it daily. The exception expires on 31 March.”
Exemption: longer-term“The field depots have no on-site staff, so the standard requiring a staffed visitor desk is replaced by camera monitoring. Approved by the security steering committee and reviewed each year.”

Making exceptions work

  • Time-boxed: every exception has an expiry date. An exception that is renewed every year without change is an undeclared exemption and should be escalated.
  • Compensating controls: the exception states what reduces the risk in the meantime, and the control owner confirms those controls operate.
  • Risk-based approval: the approver is set by the residual risk, not by the requester’s seniority.
  • Tracked centrally: exceptions appear in the risk register and in reporting, so their combined effect is visible.

Escalation

When an issue or finding misses its due date, or when the residual risk of an exception exceeds the approver’s authority, it escalates along a defined path: control owner, risk owner, the risk committee, and then senior management or the board. Escalation is not a punishment. It puts the decision with someone who has the authority to make it.

⚠️ Common mistakeTreating an open audit finding as closed because management has written an action plan. A finding closes when the action is complete and the party that raised it (or an independent function) validates that the gap is fixed.
💡 Exam TipWhen a team cannot meet a standard for a defined period, the BEST answer is usually a formal, time-limited exception with compensating controls and an expiry date. Changing the standard, ignoring the gap, or granting a permanent exemption are weaker answers.

🔎 In practice

With the default figures, insurance looks attractive: the lowest total cost of risk on the chart. Yet it leaves residual ALE above appetite, because the $300k a year of reputational loss is not insurable. Avoidance meets appetite but gives up $650k a year of savings. Add the third mitigation control and watch the answer change: three modest controls bring residual ALE within appetite at a lower total cost than walking away. That is the conversation the risk practitioner brings to Corveth’s VP of Operations, who makes the call.

💡 Exam TipThis console teaches the order of the tests. First: does the response bring residual risk within appetite? Second: among responses that do, which is most cost-effective? Third: who must approve it? Transfer never moves accountability, and acceptance above appetite always needs a higher authority than the risk owner alone.

Faculty Teaching Segment

Third-party risk is one of the most marketable GRC skills a student can bring to an interview, and it is easy to teach with documents students write and then trade.

  • Signature assignment: in round one, each team writes a three-page SOC 2-style summary for a fictional SaaS vendor: system description, scope and period, five control descriptions, two exceptions with management responses, three CUECs, and one carved-out subservice organization. In round two, teams swap summaries and act as the customer’s risk practitioner. They tier the vendor, evaluate each exception and CUEC against the customer’s risk, recommend a response (with contract clauses), and write a one-page memo to the vendor relationship owner.
  • Rubric criteria: (1) the vendor tier is justified by criticality and data sensitivity; (2) each exception is evaluated for impact on the customer’s own risk, not just restated; (3) the recommended response is cost-justified, keeps accountability with the customer, and names specific contract clauses; (4) CUECs and fourth parties are identified and assigned to an owner on the customer side.
  • Lab idea: students rebuild the Decision Console in a spreadsheet using synthetic figures for a fictional college’s learning platform vendor, then add a sixth column for a combined response (mitigate plus insure) and show whether it beats every single option. They finish by drafting a time-boxed exception request, with compensating controls and an expiry date, for one control the vendor cannot meet this term.
  • Course fit: an IT risk management, GRC, or IT audit course, or a supply chain security elective. Supports the CAE-CD Supply Chain Security (SCS), Cybersecurity Planning and Management (CPM), and Policy, Legal, Ethics, and Compliance (PLE) Knowledge Units.
🎓 Teaching noteGive each pair a one-page decision worksheet (as a file or printed handout) with a row for each of the five responses and columns for residual ALE, response cost, total cost of risk, within appetite, and approver. Pairs fill it from the console with the default settings, then again after changing one assumption you assign (a higher reputational loss, a larger deductible, a lower appetite). Debrief by asking which assumption changed the best answer and why.

Scored Knowledge Check

Test your understanding

Select the best answer for each question, then submit for your score and the rationale for every item. Like the exam, several options may be true; choose the best one.

Q1.A risk exceeds appetite. Every mitigation option analyzed costs more than the loss it would prevent, and the risk owner proposes to accept the risk. What should the risk practitioner do NEXT?

Question 1 options
Correct: C. Accepting risk above appetite is possible but requires approval above the risk owner’s normal authority and proper documentation. Implementing an uneconomic control ignores the analysis, reporting to audit is not the escalation path, and recording the acceptance as requested skips the required approval.

Q2.Corveth buys a cyber insurance policy that covers breach response and notification costs for incidents at its telematics vendor. What remains with Corveth?

Question 2 options
Correct: A. Insurance transfers part of the financial impact. Accountability to customers and regulators, and reputational harm, stay with the enterprise. The deductible is retained too, but it is only part of what remains. The insurer never owns the risk or the vendor’s controls.

Q3.Which is the PRIMARY criterion for selecting a risk response?

Question 3 options
Correct: D. A response must bring residual risk within appetite and be cost-justified. The cheapest response may not reach appetite, more controls are not automatically better, and peer practice does not reflect this enterprise’s objectives or appetite.

Q4.A critical vendor’s SOC 2 Type II report contains an exception: production access reviews were not performed for half of the period. What should the risk practitioner do FIRST?

Question 4 options
Correct: B. The practitioner first determines what the exception means for the enterprise’s own risk, considering CUECs, compensating controls, and the vendor’s remediation. Termination is premature, an independent report can still contain significant exceptions, and a SOC 1 covers controls relevant to financial reporting, not this security concern.

Q5.A plant system cannot receive a required security patch for 90 days because the equipment vendor has not certified it. What is the BEST way to handle the deviation from the patching standard?

Question 5 options
Correct: D. A temporary inability to comply calls for a formal exception: assessed, approved, supported by compensating controls such as network isolation, and set to expire. An exemption is for longer-term exclusions, weakening the standard for everyone overreacts to one case, and a finding is an assessment result, not a way to manage an approved deviation.

Q6.The risk owner has chosen to mitigate the telematics vendor risk with phishing-resistant MFA on the vendor portal. Who is accountable for ensuring that this control operates effectively day to day?

Question 6 options
Correct: A. The control owner is accountable for a control’s design and operation. The practitioner advises and monitors, internal audit provides independent assurance, and even when a vendor performs part of the work, accountability for the control stays with a named owner at Corveth.

Q7.Corveth is negotiating a contract with a new critical vendor. Which clause MOST directly supports ongoing monitoring of the vendor’s security?

Question 7 options
Correct: C. Right to audit, assurance reports, and timely breach notification give the enterprise continuing visibility into the vendor’s controls. Termination for convenience supports exit, not monitoring, and the other two clauses are commercial terms.

Q8.Which situation is BEST handled as an exemption rather than an exception?

Question 8 options
Correct: B. An exemption is a formally approved, longer-term exclusion from a requirement that does not fit the situation, reviewed periodically. The patching delay is a time-boxed exception, the audit result is a finding, and the failed backup is an issue to resolve.
