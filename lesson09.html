Day 5, Afternoon • Domain 4B + Capstone

Information Security Principles and Capstone

The last lesson closes Domain 4 with the security principles, frameworks, awareness programs, and privacy rules that every risk practitioner relies on. Then it pulls the whole workshop together: a capstone case that crosses all four domains, a tabletop exercise you can facilitate, an exam strategy for the final weeks, and a map from the CRISC outline to the courses you teach.

Session: Day 5, 1:00 to 4:30 Duration: ~3.5 hrs Exam Weight: 20% Knowledge Check: 8 Questions

Overview

Principles first, then put it all together

Domain 4B is short on the outline but long on reach. Security concepts such as least privilege and zero trust shape control design in Domain 3. Frameworks such as ISO/IEC 27001 and NIST CSF 2.0 give risk programs a common language. Awareness training is the control that touches every employee. Privacy principles decide what an organization may do with personal data at all, before any question of how to protect it.

The second half of the lesson is integration. The capstone case asks you to work one organization through governance, assessment, response, and technology in a single sitting, then run a tabletop exercise on it, which is Task 24 in the CRISC outline. The lesson ends with a study plan built from your own readiness and a curriculum map for your program.

⚖️ Think like a risk practitionerPrinciples and frameworks are tools for a business decision, not ends in themselves. When a question asks which framework or principle to adopt, the best answer starts from the organization’s objectives, obligations, and risk appetite. When it asks about a tabletop, the best answer starts from the objectives of the exercise, not the scenario script.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Explain core security concepts (CIA, authenticity, non-repudiation, least privilege, defense in depth, and zero trust) and how they guide control choices. Bloom: Understand
  • Compare ISO/IEC 27001:2022, NIST CSF 2.0, and the CIS Controls, and select the right one for a stated business need. Bloom: Analyze

  • Apply privacy principles to a processing activity and decide when a data protection impact assessment is required. Bloom: Apply
  • Plan a tabletop exercise with objectives, injects, roles, and an after-action report, and build a personal exam study plan. Bloom: Create
  • Design a curriculum map that places CRISC domains in your program’s courses, with a tabletop activity for one of them. Bloom: Create

Security Concepts

Security concepts are the shared vocabulary between the risk function and the security team. A risk scenario names which property is harmed. A control is chosen because it protects that property. When a risk practitioner says “this is an integrity risk,” the conversation narrows to the right controls straight away.

PropertyMeaningExample of harmTypical controls
ConfidentialityOnly authorized parties can read the informationCustomer list leaked to a competitorAccess control, encryption, classification, DLP
IntegrityInformation and systems are accurate and unaltered without authorizationSupplier bank details changed to divert paymentsChange control, hashing, segregation of duties, input validation
AvailabilityAuthorized users can use the information when neededOrdering system down for two daysRedundancy, backups, DR, capacity management
AuthenticityA party or message is genuinely who or what it claims to beFake invoice from a spoofed supplier domainStrong authentication, email authentication, certificates
Non-repudiationA party cannot credibly deny an actionManager denies approving a large transferDigital signatures, protected audit logs

Design principles

  • Least privilege and need to know: give each person and process only the access the task requires, and only to the specific information it requires. Least privilege limits the damage a compromised account can do.
  • Segregation of duties: split critical tasks so that no single person can both commit and conceal an error or fraud. Where staff numbers make this impossible, compensate with review and monitoring.
  • Defense in depth: layer controls of different kinds (administrative, technical, physical; preventive, detective, corrective) so that one failure does not expose the asset.
  • Zero trust: NIST SP 800-207 (2020) describes an architecture in which network location grants no trust. Each request to a resource is authenticated and authorized using identity, device health, and context, and access is granted per session. Zero trust is a strategy carried out over years, not a product.
  • Fail secure and simplicity: systems should fail to a safe state, and simpler designs are easier to secure and test.

Security Frameworks and Standards

Frameworks give a security program its structure and give stakeholders a way to compare it with others. They are not interchangeable. Some are certifiable management systems, some are outcome taxonomies, and some are prioritized control lists. The risk practitioner’s job is to recommend the one that fits the organization’s obligations and maturity, and to make sure the program is driven by risk rather than by checklist.

ISO/IEC 27001:2022

  • What it is: the international standard for an information security management system (ISMS). An organization can be certified against it by an accredited body.
  • Clauses 4 to 10: the management system requirements: context, leadership, planning (including risk assessment and treatment), support, operation, performance evaluation, and improvement.
  • Annex A: 93 controls in four themes: organizational, people, physical, and technological. ISO/IEC 27002:2022 gives implementation guidance for each.
  • Statement of Applicability (SoA): lists every Annex A control, whether it is included or excluded and why, and its status. Controls are selected by the risk treatment process, not adopted wholesale.
  • Risk link: ISO/IEC 27005:2022 gives guidance on the information security risk management that the ISMS requires.

NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (February 2024)

FunctionOutcome focusRisk practitioner connection
GovernStrategy, expectations, policy, roles, oversight, and supply chain risk managementAppetite, ownership, and accountability (Domain 1)
IdentifyAssets, risk assessment, and improvementAsset inventory and risk assessment (Domain 2)
ProtectSafeguards: identity, awareness, data security, platform security, resilienceControl design (Domain 3B)
DetectFinding and analyzing possible attacks and compromisesMonitoring and KRIs (Domain 3C)
RespondActions on a detected incidentIncident response and escalation
RecoverRestoring assets and operationsResilience and DR (Domain 4A)

CSF 2.0 added the Govern function and broadened its audience from critical infrastructure to organizations of any size or sector. Organizations use Profiles (current and target) to describe where they are and where they want to be, and Tiers to describe the rigor of their risk governance and management practices.

CIS Critical Security Controls v8.1

Eighteen prioritized controls, from enterprise asset inventory to penetration testing, with safeguards grouped into three Implementation Groups. IG1 is often described as essential cyber hygiene for smaller organizations; IG2 and IG3 add safeguards for more complex environments and more capable attackers. The CIS Controls suit organizations that need a practical starting point more than a certifiable system.

Choose by needCustomers demand a certificate: ISO/IEC 27001. The board wants a common outcome language and a target profile: NIST CSF 2.0. A small IT team needs a prioritized to-do list: CIS Controls IG1. Federal systems: NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5 under the RMF.
Use them togetherMany organizations run an ISO/IEC 27001 ISMS, report to the board in CSF 2.0 terms, and use the CIS Controls or SP 800-53 as the control catalog. Published mappings between them make this practical.
💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks which framework an organization should adopt, the BEST answer is the one that meets its business and regulatory requirements, not the most comprehensive one. When it asks what decides which ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A controls apply, the answer is the risk assessment and treatment, recorded in the Statement of Applicability.

Security and Risk Awareness and Training

Awareness is the only control that every employee operates. It works when it changes what people do, not when it produces completion certificates. A risk-aware culture goes further: people recognize risk in their own decisions, report problems early, and know that raising a concern will be welcomed rather than punished.

Role-based training

AudienceWhat they needFormat that works
Board and executivesRisk appetite, top risk scenarios, their oversight duties, disclosure obligationsShort briefings with business scenarios; tabletop exercises
Managers and risk ownersHow to own, accept, and escalate risk; exception handlingCase discussions using the organization’s own register
Developers and engineersSecure coding, threat modeling, pipeline controlsHands-on labs on their own languages and tools
Privileged administratorsPrivileged access rules, change control, loggingProcedure walkthroughs with attestation
All staffPhishing and social engineering, data handling, reportingShort, frequent modules and realistic simulations

Measuring behavior change

  • Activity measures (completion rates, hours delivered) show that training happened. They say nothing about risk.
  • Behavior measures show whether people act differently: the rate at which staff report suspicious messages, time from receipt to report, repeat clickers, policy exceptions requested, and data-handling errors found in reviews.
  • Outcome measures connect to risk: incidents caused by human error, losses from fraud attempts, and audit findings on handling. These move slowly, so pair them with behavior measures as leading indicators.
  • Reference: NIST SP 800-50 Rev. 1, Building a Cybersecurity and Privacy Learning Program (2024), covers program design, role-based training, and evaluation.

🔎 In practice

Halloway Foods reported 98 percent training completion for two years while invoice fraud attempts kept succeeding. The risk function replaced the completion KPI with two KRIs: the share of simulated fraud emails reported within an hour, and the number of supplier bank-detail changes made without a call-back check. The first quarter’s numbers were uncomfortable, and they were the first numbers that pointed to a fix.

💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks for the BEST indicator that awareness training is effective, choose a behavior measure (such as reporting rates or reduced incidents caused by staff) over completion rates or test scores. When it asks for the MOST effective way to build a risk-aware culture, look for visible tone from leadership and safe channels for reporting.

Data Privacy and Data Protection Principles

Security asks whether data is protected. Privacy asks whether the organization should be collecting and using it at all, and on what terms. A system can be perfectly secure and still violate privacy law, for example by keeping personal data longer than its purpose requires. The risk practitioner treats privacy obligations as legal and regulatory requirements in the risk profile and makes sure privacy risk to individuals is assessed, not only risk to the organization.

GDPR Article 5 principleIn plain termsTypical control
Lawfulness, fairness, transparencyHave a legal basis, do not mislead, and tell people what you doPrivacy notices; records of processing
Purpose limitationUse data only for the purposes stated when it was collectedPurpose register; review of new uses
Data minimizationCollect only what the purpose needsForm and field reviews; privacy by default
AccuracyKeep data correct and up to dateCorrection processes; data quality checks
Storage limitationKeep identifiable data no longer than neededRetention schedule; automated deletion
Integrity and confidentialityProtect data with appropriate securityAccess control; encryption; monitoring
AccountabilityBe able to demonstrate compliance with all of the abovePolicies, DPIAs, training, audit evidence
  • OECD Privacy Guidelines: eight principles first adopted in 1980 and revised in 2013 (collection limitation, data quality, purpose specification, use limitation, security safeguards, openness, individual participation, and accountability). Many national laws trace back to them.
  • Privacy by design and by default: privacy built into requirements and architecture, with the most protective settings as the default. GDPR Article 25 makes this a legal requirement.
  • DPIA: GDPR Article 35 requires a data protection impact assessment before processing that is likely to result in high risk to individuals, such as large-scale profiling, systematic monitoring, or large-scale processing of sensitive data. The DPIA describes the processing, assesses necessity and proportionality, assesses risks to individuals, and sets mitigating measures.
  • Data subject rights: access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability, objection, and rights related to automated decision-making. Each needs a process, a deadline, and an owner.
  • Breach notification: under GDPR, the controller notifies the supervisory authority without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware of a reportable breach. Other laws set their own clocks, so the incident plan must list them.
🔖 NoteFor U.S. teaching contexts, pair GDPR with a sector example such as HIPAA for health data or FERPA for student records, and with the NIST Privacy Framework 1.0 as a voluntary structure. The principles carry over even where the legal details differ.
💡 Exam TipWhen a new project will process personal data in a high-risk way, the FIRST step is a privacy or data protection impact assessment, before design is final. When a question contrasts privacy and security, remember that encryption protects confidentiality but does nothing for purpose limitation, minimization, or retention.

Capstone Case and Tabletop Facilitation

The capstone pulls the four domains into one case. Work it in teams of four, each person leading one domain, and then run the tabletop exercise that follows. The case is fictional and continues the Halloway Foods story from Lesson 8.

🔎 Capstone case: Halloway Foods, eighteen months later

Halloway Foods has moved finance and order processing to a SaaS ERP and launched its generative AI customer assistant. Grocery chains now place 70 percent of orders through the ERP’s customer portal. On a Monday morning, the ERP provider announces that a managed-services partner it uses was hit by ransomware and that customer portals are offline “for an undetermined period.” At the same time, the AI assistant, which pulls order status from the ERP, starts telling customers their orders have shipped when they have not. The risk register lists ERP concentration risk as accepted by the CFO a year ago, with a KRI on provider uptime that was never connected to a data source.

DomainCapstone questionEvidence of a strong answer
1 GovernanceWho owns the decisions today, and was the original risk acceptance within appetite?Names the CFO as risk owner, the IT director as control owner, and the board’s role; checks the acceptance against the stated appetite for order-processing outages
2 Risk AssessmentWhat scenarios are now in play, and how big are they?Separate scenarios for the outage (availability), the fourth-party breach (confidentiality of customer data), and the assistant’s wrong answers (integrity); impact from the BIA’s 8-hour MTD
3 Response and ReportingWhat responses are needed now and later, and how should this be reported?Immediate manual order process; switch the assistant off for order status; contract review; a working KRI with thresholds; a short board report with decisions requested
4 Technology and SecurityWhat technology and security gaps allowed this?No tested exit or fallback plan; AI assistant without output validation or a kill switch tied to source health; third-party assurance that did not cover fourth parties

Facilitating the tabletop (Task 24)

A tabletop exercise tests plans and decisions through discussion, not live systems. It is cheap, safe, and revealing. The quality of a tabletop depends on preparation: clear objectives, realistic injects, the right people in the room, and a written after-action report that leads to change.

  • Objectives (set first): (1) confirm that the order-processing fallback can be activated within the 8-hour MTD; (2) test the escalation path from the risk owner to the board; (3) validate the decision to suspend the AI assistant and who may make it; (4) check that customer and regulatory notification duties are identified within the required time frames.
  • Ground rules: no-fault discussion; use the plans as written, not as remembered; decisions are made by the role that owns them; the facilitator may pause to ask “who decides?” at any point.
  • Scope and length: 90 minutes, five injects, one scenario. Keep it small enough to finish and discuss.

FacilitatorRisk practitionerDelivers injects, keeps time, asks probing questions, does not make decisions
Risk ownerCFODecides on fallback activation, spending, and risk acceptance

After-action report

  • Hot wash (immediately after): 15 minutes in the room. What went well, what was unclear, what surprised people.
  • Report sections: exercise objectives; summary of the scenario; for each objective, what was expected, what happened, and whether it was met; gaps found; corrective actions, each with an owner and a due date; and updates required to the risk register, plans, and KRIs.
  • Follow-through: corrective actions enter the risk action plan and are monitored like any other. An after-action report with no owners is a record, not a response.
💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks what to define FIRST when planning a tabletop, the answer is the exercise objectives. When it asks for the MOST important output, it is the after-action report with corrective actions and owners. The facilitator guides; the participants who own the decisions make them.

Exam Strategy

The CRISC exam has 150 multiple-choice questions in four hours, scored on a scale of 200 to 800 with 450 to pass. That is 1.6 minutes per question. Most candidates who know the material and fail do so because they answer as technicians or auditors instead of as risk practitioners. Confirm all logistics in the current ISACA Exam Candidate Guide before you book.

Question tactics

  • Read the last sentence first. Find the qualifier (FIRST, BEST, MOST, PRIMARY, GREATEST concern) and the role (risk practitioner, risk owner, management) before reading the scenario.
  • Find the process step. If the scenario has not yet identified or assessed the risk, an answer that responds to it is premature. If it has, an answer that assesses again may be too slow.
  • Prefer business over technology. Answers tied to business objectives, ownership, and appetite usually beat answers that name a specific tool or configuration.
  • Respect ownership. The practitioner advises, recommends, facilitates, and reports. The risk owner accepts or decides. The board sets appetite. Answers that put the practitioner in the owner’s chair are usually wrong.
  • Eliminate the extremes. “Immediately shut down,” “ignore,” and “replace the entire system” are rarely the best answer.
  • Pace with checkpoints. About 50 questions by 1:20, 100 by 2:40, and all 150 by 4:00, with a few minutes to spare. Flag and move on rather than stall on one item.

The final week

Interactive: Readiness and Study Planner

Rate your readiness in each CRISC domain from 0 to 100, ideally using your most recent practice-question score for that domain. Then set the study hours you can commit and the weeks until your exam. The planner weights each domain by its share of the exam (D1 26%, D2 22%, D3 32%, D4 20%) to compute a weighted readiness score. It allocates study hours in proportion to weight × (100 − readiness), so a large gap in a heavily weighted domain gets the most time. The final week is held back for a full timed practice exam and review. The chart draws each domain as a bar whose thickness reflects its exam weight, with the gap to a readiness target of 75 marked in copper.

Running a tabletop in class

  • Prepare (one week before): write two or three objectives, a one-page scenario, five inject cards, and a role card for each seat. Print them, or share them as files, so every student holds the material.
  • Run (50 minutes): 5 minutes for roles and ground rules, 30 minutes for injects at 6-minute intervals, 15 minutes for the hot wash. One student per team is scribe and one is evaluator with the objectives checklist.
  • Close (homework): each team submits an after-action report from a template with objective-by-objective findings and three corrective actions with owners and dates.
  • Signature assignment: teams design and run a tabletop exercise for a fictional organization from this workshop (Larchmont Community Health, Brightwater Credit Union, Corveth Logistics, Penmore Technical College, or Halloway Foods). The package includes objectives, a scenario, at least five injects, role cards, an evaluator checklist, and the after-action report from their own run.
  • Rubric criteria: (1) objectives are specific and testable; (2) injects escalate realistically and each tests a named decision; (3) roles separate decision owners from advisors and the facilitator; (4) the after-action report links each gap to a corrective action with an owner, a due date, and a risk register update.
  • Lab idea: build a simple exercise tracker in a spreadsheet: inject times, decisions logged by the scribe, time taken per decision, and objective status. Students run a second team’s exercise and compare decision times against the MTD in the scenario. Use only synthetic scenario data.
  • Course fit: a GRC or IT risk capstone, or the final module of a security management course. It supports the CAE-CD Knowledge Units Cybersecurity Planning and Management (CPM); Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery (BCD); Policy, Legal, Ethics, and Compliance (PLE); and IA Compliance (IAC).
🎓 Teaching noteUse the study planner twice in class. First, have students set sliders from a short diagnostic quiz and save the week-by-week plan as a PDF from the browser’s print dialog. Second, three weeks later, have them repeat it and compare the two plans. The change in weighted readiness, and the reasons for it, make a strong reflective writing prompt.

Scored Knowledge Check

Test your understanding

Select the best answer for each question, then submit for your score and the rationale for every item. Like the exam, several options may be true; choose the best one.

Q1.A distributor wants to make sure that managers who approve large supplier payments cannot later deny having approved them. Which control BEST meets this need?

Question 1 options
Correct: B. Non-repudiation needs proof tied to one individual, which a digital signature with a private key under that person’s sole control provides. Encryption protects confidentiality, a shared account destroys individual accountability, and a hash shows integrity but not who approved.

Q2.An organization implementing ISO/IEC 27001:2022 has decided which Annex A controls apply to its ISMS and which do not. Where should these decisions and their justifications be recorded?

Question 2 options
Correct: D. The Statement of Applicability lists each Annex A control, whether it is included, the justification, and its status. The scope statement defines the boundaries of the ISMS, the risk register records risks rather than control applicability, and the policy sets direction.

Q3.Leadership asks for evidence that the security awareness program is reducing social engineering risk. Which measure provides the BEST evidence?

Question 3 options
Correct: A. Reporting rates measure a change in behavior, which is what reduces risk. Completion counts and sessions delivered show activity, and quiz scores show short-term knowledge, but none of them show that people act differently.

Q4.A retailer plans a new analytics program that will profile customers’ purchasing behavior on a large scale to personalize offers. What should the risk practitioner recommend FIRST?

Question 4 options
Correct: C. Large-scale profiling is the kind of high-risk processing that calls for a DPIA before processing begins. The DPIA will determine what notice, security, and other measures are needed. Encryption, notice updates, and insurance are responses chosen before the risk is assessed.

Q5.A tabletop exercise revealed that no one knew who could authorize switching off a customer-facing AI assistant. What is the MOST important output of the exercise?

Question 5 options
Correct: D. The value of a tabletop is the gaps it finds and the changes that follow. An after-action report assigns each corrective action to an owner with a date. Recordings and harder scenarios are secondary, and scoring individuals discourages the open discussion that exercises depend on.

Q6.A board asks which NIST CSF 2.0 function covers the organization’s cybersecurity risk management strategy, roles, policy, and oversight. Which function should the risk practitioner point to?

Question 6 options
Correct: B. Govern, added in CSF 2.0, covers strategy, expectations, policy, roles, oversight, and supply chain risk management. Identify covers assets and risk assessment, Protect covers safeguards, and Respond covers actions on detected incidents.

Q7.Remote staff connect through a VPN that gives full network access once a user logs in. A risk assessment flags lateral movement after account compromise as a high risk. Which approach BEST addresses it?

Question 7 options
Correct: C. Per-request authorization to specific applications is the zero trust approach and directly removes the broad network access that enables lateral movement. Stronger encryption protects the tunnel, longer passwords make compromise somewhat harder but do not limit what a compromised account can reach, and an IDS detects rather than prevents.

Q8.A risk practitioner has been asked to facilitate a tabletop exercise on a third-party outage. What should the practitioner do FIRST?

Question 8 options
Correct: A. Objectives decide everything else: which injects to write, who must attend, and what the after-action report will measure. Writing injects or inviting people first risks an exercise that tests nothing in particular.
