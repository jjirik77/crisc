Day 4, Morning • Domain 3B

Control Design and Implementation

A risk response is only a promise until a control carries it out. Domain 3B covers how controls are classified, which frameworks supply them, how they are designed and selected, how they are put into service, and how a risk practitioner proves that they work: first by design, then in operation.

Session: Day 4, 9:00 to 12:30 Duration: ~3.5 hrs Exam Weight: 32% Knowledge Check: 8 Questions

Overview

From a chosen response to a working control

Lesson 5 ended with a decision: mitigate this risk. This lesson turns that decision into controls. A good control set is not the longest list. It is a balanced portfolio that prevents what it can, detects what it cannot prevent, and corrects and recovers when both fail, at a cost the business can justify.

The lesson moves through control types and functions, the major control frameworks, design and selection, implementation, control analysis, and control testing. The running case is Penmore Technical College, whose student information system (SIS) faces a rising account-takeover risk.

⚖️ Think like a risk practitionerThe risk practitioner recommends controls and evaluates whether they work. The risk owner decides which response to fund, and the control owner operates the control day to day. When a question asks what a well-designed control must have FIRST, look for a clear objective tied to the risk and a named owner, not a product.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Classify controls by function (preventive, detective, corrective, deterrent, recovery, compensating) and by nature (administrative, technical, physical). Bloom: Understand
  • Compare NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5, ISO/IEC 27002:2022, CIS Controls v8.1, and COBIT 2019 as sources of controls. Bloom: Analyze
  • Select a cost-justified, balanced control portfolio that brings residual risk within appetite. Bloom: Evaluate
  • Distinguish design effectiveness from operating effectiveness and explain why both matter. Bloom: Understand
  • Choose the test method (inquiry, observation, inspection, reperformance) that gives enough assurance for a given control. Bloom: Apply
  • Design a control test workpaper assignment, with a rubric, for students in your own course. Bloom: Create

Control Types and Functions

A control is any policy, procedure, practice, or mechanism that modifies risk. Two independent questions classify every control. First, what does it do relative to the risk event: stop it, discourage it, find it, fix it, or restore after it? That is the control's function. Second, how is it delivered: through people and rules, through technology, or through the physical environment? That is the control's nature, sometimes called its category or type.

FunctionWhat it doesPenmore SIS example
DeterrentDiscourages a threat actor by raising the perceived cost or certainty of consequences.A sign-in banner stating that access is monitored and misuse is prosecuted.
PreventiveStops the event from occurring.Multi-factor authentication (MFA) on every SIS sign-in.
DetectiveIdentifies the event during or after it occurs.Alerts on sign-ins from two distant locations within an hour.
CorrectiveLimits the damage and removes the cause.An account-takeover playbook: disable the session, reset credentials, review changes.
RecoveryRestores normal operations and data after the event.Restoring altered grade and aid records from a tested backup.
CompensatingMeets the intent of a required control that cannot be implemented as written.A second-person review of aid bank-account changes while a legacy module cannot enforce MFA.

Some lists add a directive function for controls that tell people what to do, such as an acceptable use policy. Many controls serve more than one function. Security awareness training is mainly preventive, yet it also deters. An access recertification detects inappropriate access that already exists, then triggers its removal, which is corrective.

The nature of a control

  • Administrative (managerial or procedural): policies, standards, procedures, training, segregation of duties, background checks, and reviews performed by people. They set expectations and depend on consistent human performance.
  • Technical (logical): controls enforced by hardware, software, or firmware, such as MFA, encryption, firewalls, and automated alerting. They act consistently once configured, which also makes them easier to test.
  • Physical: controls on the physical environment, such as locks, badges, guards, cameras, and environmental protection for server rooms.
Function answers “when”Before the event (deter, prevent), during or after it (detect), or after it (correct, recover). A portfolio needs coverage across that timeline.
Nature answers “how”People and rules, technology, or the physical environment. A portfolio that relies on only one nature has a single point of failure.

Control Frameworks and Standards

Practitioners rarely invent controls from nothing. Control frameworks give a tested catalog, a shared vocabulary, and a way to show regulators and auditors that coverage is complete. The choice of framework follows the organization's legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements and its business context, not personal preference.

FrameworkStructureBest use
NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5A catalog of security and privacy controls in 20 families, such as AC (Access Control) and AU (Audit and Accountability). Baselines (low, moderate, high, and privacy) are published separately in SP 800-53B.U.S. federal systems and contractors, and any organization that wants a deep, detailed catalog. Used with the NIST RMF (SP 800-37 Rev. 2).
ISO/IEC 27002:202293 controls in 4 themes: organizational, people, physical, and technological. Each control carries attributes, such as control type (preventive, detective, corrective) and cybersecurity concept.Guidance for the Annex A controls of an ISO/IEC 27001:2022 ISMS. Common in international and certified environments.
CIS Controls v8.118 prioritized controls broken into safeguards, grouped into Implementation Groups IG1, IG2, and IG3. IG1 is essential cyber hygiene; each higher group adds safeguards.Small and mid-sized organizations that need a prioritized, practical starting point.
COBIT 201940 governance and management objectives in 5 domains: EDM, APO, BAI, DSS, and MEA.Governance of enterprise information and technology. It frames controls in terms of business goals and assigns accountability.

Other sources fill specific needs. NIST CSF 2.0 describes outcomes in six functions (Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover) rather than prescribing controls, and organizations map controls from a catalog to those outcomes. PCI DSS v4.0 is a contractual requirement for organizations that handle payment card data. Sector rules, such as the HIPAA Security Rule, add their own safeguards.

Working with more than one framework

  • Crosswalks (control mapping): one internal control can satisfy requirements in several frameworks. Mapping avoids building and testing the same control twice.
  • A common control set: many organizations keep one internal control library with references to each external framework. Testing evidence is then gathered once and reused.
  • Tailoring: no framework fits as published. Controls are scoped to the systems and risks in play and tailored to the organization's size and context.
📋 Note on ISO/IEC 27002:2022The 2022 edition replaced the 2013 structure of 14 clauses and 114 controls with 93 controls in 4 themes. It also introduced attribute tags, so the same control list can be filtered by control type, by security property, or by CSF-style concept. Older study materials that cite 114 controls describe the previous edition.
💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks what should drive the selection of a control framework, choose the organization's business, regulatory, and contractual requirements. A framework is a tool for meeting those requirements, not an end in itself.
🔎 In practice

Penmore Technical College receives federal student aid, so it must protect student financial data under the GLBA Safeguards Rule, and it runs a small IT team. The risk practitioner recommends CIS Controls v8.1 IG1 and IG2 as the working baseline, mapped to NIST CSF 2.0 for board reporting. One internal control, “MFA on all remote and SIS access,” maps to a CIS safeguard, a CSF Protect outcome, and the Safeguards Rule's MFA requirement at the same time.

3

Control Design and Selection

Control design starts with the risk scenario, not the catalog. The practitioner asks which part of the scenario the control addresses: the threat, the vulnerability, the likelihood of the event, or the size of the loss. A control that addresses none of these is overhead, however good it looks on paper.

Design principles

  • Clear objective: each control states the risk it addresses and what success looks like. “Ensure only current staff can change aid records” is testable; “secure the SIS” is not.
  • Defense in depth: several independent layers, so one failure does not expose the asset.
  • Least privilege and segregation of duties: no single person can complete a sensitive action alone or reach more than the role needs.
  • Fail secure: when the control breaks, it denies access rather than opening up.
  • Automation where it is reliable: automated controls run the same way every time and are cheaper to test. Manual controls suit judgment-heavy tasks, such as reviewing unusual transactions.
  • Evidence by design: the control leaves a record (log, ticket, signed review) that shows it ran. A control without evidence cannot be shown to work.

Selection criteria

CriterionQuestion to ask
Risk reductionHow much does it lower likelihood or impact for this scenario?
CostWhat is the total cost of ownership: purchase, staff time, maintenance, and testing? Does it stay below the value of the risk it reduces?
ComplianceIs it required by law, regulation, or contract regardless of cost-benefit?
FeasibilityCan it work with the current technology, staff, and culture?
Operational impactWhat does it do to users and business processes? A control people route around is not a control.
BalanceDoes the portfolio still cover deterrence, prevention, detection, correction, and recovery?

Key and non-key controls

A key control is one whose failure alone would leave the risk outside appetite. Key controls get the most rigorous design, the clearest ownership, and the most frequent testing. Non-key controls add depth, but the risk would remain acceptable without any single one of them. Identifying key controls lets the organization spend testing effort where it matters.

💡 Exam TipWhen two answers both reduce the risk, prefer the one that addresses the root cause in the scenario at a cost proportionate to the risk. A control that costs more than the loss it prevents is not justified unless law or contract requires it.
🔎 In practice

Penmore's first draft response was “buy a SIEM.” The risk practitioner reframed the scenario: attackers reuse staff passwords stolen by phishing, then change aid deposit accounts. MFA addresses the stolen-password vulnerability directly and costs less. Impossible-travel alerting detects the sessions MFA misses. A playbook and tested restores cover what happens next. The SIEM stays on the roadmap, but it is no longer the first purchase.

4

Control Implementation

Implementation is where good designs fail most often. A control that is switched on without a tested change, a trained owner, or documentation tends to drift, break, or be bypassed within months.

  • Change management: new controls are changes to the environment. They go through the normal change process: a request, impact analysis, testing in a non-production environment, approval, a scheduled release, and a back-out plan.
  • Ownership: every control has a named control owner who is accountable for operating it and producing evidence. The control owner is often different from the risk owner, who is accountable for the risk the control addresses.
  • Documentation: a control narrative records the objective, the risk it addresses, the owner, the frequency (continuous, daily, quarterly), the procedure, and the evidence it leaves. This narrative becomes the basis for testing.
  • Transition: pilot groups, phased rollout, or parallel running reduce disruption. For MFA, enrolling staff by department with a help desk ready is safer than a single cut-over.
  • Training and communication: people who operate or are affected by a control need to know what changes and why.
  • Post-implementation review: after go-live, confirm the control works as designed, measure user impact, and record lessons learned.
RoleAccountable forPenmore example
Risk ownerThe risk and the decision on how to respond to it.Vice President of Enrollment and Student Services
Control ownerOperating the control and keeping evidence.Identity and access manager (MFA); financial aid director (bank-change review)
Risk practitionerAdvising on design, monitoring, and evaluating effectiveness.The college's IT risk analyst
Internal auditIndependent assurance that controls work.The college's internal audit function (third line)
💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks who is accountable for making sure a control operates as intended, the answer is the control owner. The risk owner decides on the response, and the risk practitioner evaluates and reports, but neither runs the control.
🔎 In practice

Penmore's MFA rollout reached the financial aid office last, because its legacy aid module could not accept the new sign-in flow until a vendor update. The risk practitioner documented the gap, the financial aid director agreed to a time-boxed compensating control (second-person review of every bank-account change), and the exception was logged with a review date in the risk register.

5

Control Analysis

Control analysis asks whether the current controls are enough, whether they are the right ones, and how mature they are. It compares what exists with what the risk requires.

Gap analysis

A gap analysis compares the current state of controls with a target state. The target can come from a framework baseline, a regulation, or the risk scenario itself. Each gap is recorded with its risk, an owner, and a remediation plan, and significant gaps go into the risk register and the risk action plan (Lesson 7).

Design versus operating effectiveness

Design effectivenessOperating effectiveness
QuestionIf this control runs as described, would it meet its objective?Did it actually run as described, consistently, throughout the period?
TimingPoint in timeOver a period (for example, a quarter or a year)
Typical evidenceControl narrative, configuration, walkthrough of one transactionA sample of instances across the period, logs, reperformance
Deficiency typeDesign deficiency: the control is missing or would not work even if followed.Operating deficiency: a sound control was not performed, or was performed wrongly.

The order matters. Testing operating effectiveness of a poorly designed control wastes effort, because a control that would not work even when followed perfectly cannot become effective by being followed more often. Design is assessed first, then operation.

Maturity and rationalization

  • Capability and maturity: COBIT 2019 rates process capability on a scale from 0 (incomplete) to 5 (optimizing). Moving from ad hoc to defined and measured processes makes controls more repeatable.
  • Control rationalization: remove or merge redundant controls that address the same risk with no added benefit. Fewer, stronger controls are easier to own and test.
  • Control dependencies: automated controls rely on IT general controls, such as change management and access to the configuration. If those fail, the automated control can be altered without anyone noticing.
💡 Exam TipWhen a question says a control is performed every time but the risk still materializes, suspect a design deficiency. When a control is well designed but evidence shows it was skipped in some periods, that is an operating deficiency.
6

Control Testing

Testing produces the evidence that a control is designed well and operating as intended. Four basic methods are used, alone or together. They differ in how much assurance they give.

MethodWhat the tester doesAssuranceExample
InquiryAsks people how the control works.Weakest. Never sufficient alone.Interview the identity manager about MFA enforcement.
ObservationWatches the control being performed.Moderate, but only for the moment observed. People may behave differently when watched.Watch a help-desk analyst verify identity before a password reset.
InspectionExamines documents, records, configurations, or logs.Good. Shows evidence that the control ran.Inspect signed quarterly access reviews for a sample of quarters.
ReperformanceIndependently executes the control and compares the result.Strongest.Attempt six failed sign-ins on a test account and confirm the lockout.

A walkthrough traces a single transaction through the process from start to finish. It combines inquiry, observation, and inspection to confirm that the control is designed as documented and has been implemented. A walkthrough supports design effectiveness. It is not, by itself, a test of operating effectiveness over a period.

Sampling

  • Population first: confirm that the population is complete before sampling. A sample drawn from an incomplete list proves nothing about the missing items.
  • Statistical sampling uses random selection and probability to draw conclusions about the whole population with stated confidence. Nonstatistical (judgmental) sampling relies on the tester's judgment and cannot be extrapolated with measured confidence.
  • Frequency drives sample size: a control that runs many times a day needs a larger sample across the period than one that runs quarterly.
  • Automated controls: once the configuration is confirmed, testing a single instance can be enough, provided IT general controls over changes to that configuration are effective for the period.

Continuous controls monitoring and who tests

Continuous controls monitoring (CCM) uses automation to test control data all the time: MFA enrollment, configuration drift, or orphaned accounts compared with the HR roster. It shifts testing from a yearly snapshot to an ongoing signal and feeds the key control indicators covered in Lesson 7. Testing can be done by the first line (control self-testing), the second line (risk and compliance functions), or the third line (internal audit). Independence increases from first to third line.

💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks which evidence gives the MOST assurance that a control is operating, choose reperformance. When the only evidence is that the control owner says it works, that is inquiry, and inquiry alone is never sufficient.
⚙️ Match method to controlReperformance fits controls a tester can safely trigger, such as lockouts, alerts, and restores. Inspection fits controls that leave records, such as reviews and approvals. Observation fits controls that only exist while performed, such as a visitor sign-in or a banner on a screen.

    Faculty Teaching Segment

    Control testing is a skill students can practice with nothing more than a control narrative, a small evidence set, and a workpaper template. Give them all three as files and ask them to reach a conclusion they can defend.

    • Signature assignment: provide a control test workpaper template and an evidence packet for three Penmore controls: MFA enforcement, quarterly access recertification, and the account lockout setting. For each, students state the control objective, judge design effectiveness, choose and justify a test method (inquiry, observation, inspection, or reperformance), test a sample of 10 items from a synthetic population of 60, record exceptions, and conclude whether the control is operating effectively.
    • Rubric criteria: (1) design and operating effectiveness are assessed separately and in the right order; (2) the test method fits the control and is stronger than inquiry alone; (3) the sample is drawn from a population confirmed to be complete, and exceptions are counted correctly; (4) the conclusion follows from the evidence and names the deficiency type and a remediation owner.
    • Lab idea: build a spreadsheet of 60 synthetic access-review records and an HR roster with a handful of leavers planted in it. Students reperform the recertification by matching the two lists, find the leavers the reviewer missed, and calculate the exception rate. Use only data you generate.
    • Course fit: an IT audit, GRC, or security management course. It supports the CAE-CD IA Compliance (IAC), Cybersecurity Planning and Management (CPM), and Cybersecurity Principles (CSP) Knowledge Units.
    🎓 Teaching noteRun the Control Portfolio Designer in pairs. Hand each pair a one-page worksheet (as a file or printout) with a blank portfolio table: control, function, nature, cost, and test method. Pairs must reach a Balanced verdict, then trade worksheets with another pair, who choose one key control and write how they would test it. Debrief by comparing portfolios that reached Balanced in different ways.

    Scored Knowledge Check

    Test your understanding

    Select the best answer for each question, then submit for your score and the rationale for every item. Like the exam, several options may be true; choose the best one.

    Q1.A college's legacy financial aid module cannot support multi-factor authentication until a vendor update next year. The risk owner wants the risk kept within appetite until then. What should the risk practitioner recommend FIRST?

    Question 1 options
    Correct: B. When a required control is not feasible, a compensating control that meets the same intent keeps the risk within appetite, and documenting it with a review date keeps it from becoming permanent. Accepting the risk ignores the owner's stated goal, replacement is disproportionate for a temporary gap, and training alone does not address stolen-password risk with comparable strength.

    Q2.A risk practitioner wants the MOST reliable evidence that an account lockout setting is operating as designed. Which test method should be used?

    Question 2 options
    Correct: D. Reperformance gives the strongest assurance because the tester executes the control and sees the result. Inquiry is the weakest evidence, a single mistyped password does not reach the threshold, and the written standard shows intended design, not operation.

    Q3.Quarterly access reviews are signed on time every quarter, yet a recent audit found many former employees with active accounts. The reviewers had been checking access against the department's own staff list instead of the HR termination records. What is the PRIMARY issue?

    Question 3 options
    Correct: A. The review is performed as described and on time, but it compares access with the wrong source, so it cannot meet its objective even when followed perfectly. That is a design deficiency. The reviews were not late, the audit finding is not a sampling problem, and deterrence does not remove leavers' access.

    Q4.A small college with a two-person IT team needs a prioritized, practical set of safeguards to start its security program. Which source is the BEST fit?

    Question 4 options
    Correct: C. CIS Controls v8.1 are prioritized, and IG1 defines essential cyber hygiene for organizations with limited resources. The 800-53 high baseline is far more than a small team can implement, COBIT 2019 addresses governance rather than prioritized safeguards, and certification is a goal, not a starting control set.

    Q5.Which statement about ISO/IEC 27002:2022 is MOST accurate?

    Question 5 options
    Correct: D. The 2022 edition reorganized the controls into 93 controls in 4 themes. A describes the 2013 edition, B describes ISO/IEC 27001, which is the certifiable standard, and C describes CIS Controls v8.1.

    Q6.A team plans to test the operating effectiveness of a new manual approval control. The risk practitioner has not yet confirmed that the control, if performed, would address the risk. What should be done FIRST?

    Question 6 options
    Correct: A. Design is assessed before operation. If the control would not address the risk even when performed perfectly, testing how consistently it was performed wastes effort. Sampling and interviews belong to later testing, and CCM is an automation choice, not a first step.

    Q7.A portfolio for an account-takeover risk includes MFA, account lockout, and a sign-in banner. Residual risk is within appetite. Which addition would MOST improve the portfolio?

    Question 7 options
    Correct: C. The portfolio is prevention and deterrence only. A detective control finds the takeovers that get past MFA, which gives the organization a chance to respond. The other options add more prevention or deterrence of the same kind and leave the detection gap open.

    Q8.Who should be accountable for performing a quarterly access recertification and keeping evidence that it was done?

    Question 8 options
    Correct: B. The control owner operates the control and keeps its evidence. The risk practitioner advises and evaluates, internal audit provides independent assurance and should not run controls it audits, and the audit committee provides oversight.
